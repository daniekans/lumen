A <Term id="matrix">matrix</Term> stores where a linear map sends the basis. The product
$A\mathbf{x}$ turns that table back into the map: it computes the image of $\mathbf{x}$. There are
two ways to read the same computation, and both are used all the time.

## By columns

Let $A$ have $m$ rows and $n$ columns $\mathbf{a}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{a}_n$, and let $\mathbf{x}$
have $n$ components. Then

$$
A\mathbf{x} = x_1\mathbf{a}_1 + x_2\mathbf{a}_2 + \cdots + x_n\mathbf{a}_n.
$$

The components of $\mathbf{x}$ are the weights of
a <Term id="linear-combination">linear combination</Term> of the columns. In
the <StepLink step="columns">plane</StepLink>, $x_1$ steps along <C c="green">$\mathbf{a}_1$</C>
and then $x_2$ steps along <C c="red">$\mathbf{a}_2$</C> reach <C c="teal">$A\mathbf{x}$</C>.
For example,

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1.5 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
= 1.5 \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + 1 \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2.5 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

## By rows

Entry $i$ of $x_1\mathbf{a}_1 + \cdots + x_n\mathbf{a}_n$ is $a_{i1}x_1 + \cdots + a_{in}x_n$:
the <Term id="dot-product">dot product</Term> of row $i$ of $A$ with $\mathbf{x}$. So, writing
$\mathbf{r}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{r}_m$ for the rows,

$$
A\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{r}_1 \cdot \mathbf{x} \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf{r}_m \cdot \mathbf{x} \end{bmatrix}.
$$

In the example, $(2, -1) \cdot (1.5, 1) = 2$ and $(1, 1) \cdot (1.5, 1) = 2.5$. The two readings
are the same sum grouped two ways. The column picture says what $A\mathbf{x}$ can reach (later:
span, column space, rank); the row picture is how it is computed by hand, and it leads to equations
and orthogonality.

## Shapes

An $m \times n$ matrix has $m$ rows and $n$ columns. $A\mathbf{x}$ needs one weight per column, so
$\mathbf{x}$ is in $\mathbb{R}^n$, and gives one entry per row, so $A\mathbf{x}$ is in $\mathbb{R}^m$:

$$
A \text{ is } m \times n: \qquad \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n \ \mapsto\ A\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^m.
$$

The <StepLink step="shapes">$2 \times 3$ example</StepLink> combines three columns of the plane, so it
maps space to the plane. When the sizes do not match, $A\mathbf{x}$ is not defined.

## Every matrix is a linear map

The column form shows that $\mathbf{x} \mapsto A\mathbf{x}$ keeps sums and multiples:

$$
A(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = \sum_j (u_j + v_j)\,\mathbf{a}_j = \sum_j u_j\mathbf{a}_j + \sum_j v_j\mathbf{a}_j = A\mathbf{u} + A\mathbf{v},
$$

and $A(c\mathbf{v}) = \sum_j c\,v_j\mathbf{a}_j = c\,A\mathbf{v}$. In the other direction,
a <Term id="linear-transformation">linear map</Term> $T$ from $\mathbb{R}^n$ to $\mathbb{R}^m$ has the
matrix with columns $T(\mathbf{e}_1), \ldots, T(\mathbf{e}_n)$, and $T(\mathbf{x}) = A\mathbf{x}$
for every $\mathbf{x}$. Matrices with $m$ rows and $n$ columns and linear maps from $\mathbb{R}^n$
to $\mathbb{R}^m$ are two descriptions of the same things, as long as both use the standard basis.

The <StepLink step="identity">identity matrix</StepLink> $I$ has the standard basis as its columns,
$1$ on the diagonal and $0$ elsewhere, so $I\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}$.

## Tables of data

Put <StepLink step="food">two foods</StepLink> in the columns, in grams per serving, with
$\mathbf{x}$ the servings:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 20 & 5 \\ 10 & 20 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1.5 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} 35 \\ 35 \end{bmatrix} \begin{matrix} \text{protein (g)} \\ \text{carbs (g)} \end{matrix}
$$

The column picture is the meal: $1.5$ servings of A plus one of B. The row picture is the label:
protein is $20 \cdot 1.5 + 5 \cdot 1$. Units check out row by row, grams per serving times
servings.

The same works for prices. Put the price per kg of three products in two stores in the rows of $P$
(euros per kg), and a basket $\mathbf{q}$ in kg:

$$
P\mathbf{q} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 12 & 1 \\ 2.5 & 14 & 1.2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0.5 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} 15 \\ 15.6 \end{bmatrix},
$$

the cost of the basket in each store, in euros. Each entry is the basket total from Vectors, one
row at a time.

## One row: a dot product

A $1 \times n$ matrix $\begin{bmatrix} w_1 & \cdots & w_n \end{bmatrix}$ has one row, so it returns
one number: $\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x}$. A dot product with a fixed $\mathbf{w}$ is a linear map
from $\mathbb{R}^n$ to the numbers, written as a matrix. The <StepLink step="dot">exam score</StepLink>
of Vectors, $\mathbf{w} = (0.5, 0.3)$ on hours studied and slept, is one. Inputs with the same
output lie on a line perpendicular to <C c="orange">$\mathbf{w}$</C>, and the outputs grow by the
same amount from one line to the next.

## A layer: $W\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}$

A <StepLink step="layer">layer</StepLink> of a neural network computes several scores at once.
Each row of $W$ holds one neuron's weights and $\mathbf{b}$ holds their constants:

$$
W\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & 0.3 \\ -0.2 & 0.4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 6 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix}
+ \begin{bmatrix} -4 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5.1 \\ 1.6 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} -4 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} 1.1 \\ 0.6 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

The first row is the exam score of Vectors. Then an activation function is applied to each entry;
that comes with neural networks.

With $\mathbf{b} \ne \mathbf{0}$ the map $\mathbf{x} \mapsto W\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}$ is not linear:
it sends $\mathbf{0}$ to <C c="yellow">$\mathbf{b}$</C>. It is a linear map followed by a shift,
an <Term id="affine-map">affine map</Term>, like Celsius to Fahrenheit. One extra coordinate makes it
linear one dimension up. Append a $1$ to $\mathbf{x}$, put $\mathbf{b}$ as a last column next to
$W$, and add a last row of zeros ending in $1$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} W & \mathbf{b} \\ 0 \ \cdots \ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{x} \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} W\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b} \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

These are homogeneous coordinates. Machine learning code often does the same thing by adding a
column of ones to the data, so the bias becomes one more weight.
