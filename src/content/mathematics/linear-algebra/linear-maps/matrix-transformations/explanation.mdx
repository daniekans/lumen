A <Term id="linear-transformation">linear map</Term> of the plane moves infinitely many points, yet
four numbers describe it completely. This item shows why, and how those four numbers are arranged
in a matrix.

## The basis decides everything

Every vector of the plane is a combination of the <Term id="standard-basis">standard basis</Term>:
$\mathbf{x} = (x, y) = x\hat{\imath} + y\hat{\jmath}$. A linear map keeps combinations, so

$$
T(\mathbf{x}) = x\,T(\hat{\imath}) + y\,T(\hat{\jmath}).
$$

Once <C c="green">$T(\hat{\imath})$</C> and <C c="red">$T(\hat{\jmath})$</C> are known, the image of
every vector follows: the weights $x$ and $y$ stay the same, only the vectors they multiply change.
The dashed path to <C c="teal">$T(\mathbf{x})$</C> shows this for $\mathbf{x} = (2, 1)$: two steps
along $T(\hat{\imath})$, then one along $T(\hat{\jmath})$. Two different linear maps that agree on
$\hat{\imath}$ and $\hat{\jmath}$ agree everywhere.

## The columns of the matrix

The <Term id="matrix">matrix</Term> of $T$ writes the two images side by side, as columns:

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
T(\hat{\imath}) = \begin{bmatrix} a \\ c \end{bmatrix}, \quad
T(\hat{\jmath}) = \begin{bmatrix} b \\ d \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Then $T(\mathbf{x}) = x \begin{bmatrix} a \\ c \end{bmatrix} + y \begin{bmatrix} b \\ d \end{bmatrix}$,
which the next lesson writes as the product $A\mathbf{x}$. The order matters: the first column is
always the image of $\hat{\imath}$.

Going both ways:

- From a picture to a matrix (<StepLink step="read">reading</StepLink>): find where $\hat{\imath}$
  and $\hat{\jmath}$ land and write those vectors as the columns.
- From a matrix to a picture (<StepLink step="draw">drawing</StepLink>): put the tip of
  $\hat{\imath}$ at the first column and the tip of $\hat{\jmath}$ at the second; the grid is
  spanned by them.

Drag the tips in any step to set the columns yourself.

## Italic type

Slanted type is a <StepLink step="italic">shear</StepLink>. The baseline stays put, so
$T(\hat{\imath}) = \hat{\imath}$, and each point moves sideways in proportion to its height:
$T(\hat{\jmath}) = (0.25, 1)$. The matrix is

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0.25 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad (x, y) \mapsto (x + 0.25\,y,\ y).
$$

## Presets

| Map                            | Matrix                                                 | What it does                                        |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Rotate by $90^\circ$           | $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$        | Turns the plane; lengths, angles and shapes stay    |
| Reflect across the $y$-axis    | $\begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$        | Flips the letter; lengths and angles stay           |
| Scale                          | $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1.5 \end{bmatrix}$       | Stretches by $2$ across and $1.5$ up                |
| Project onto $y = \frac{x}{2}$ | $\begin{bmatrix} 0.8 & 0.4 \\ 0.4 & 0.2 \end{bmatrix}$ | Flattens the plane onto a line; it cannot be undone |

A rotation is a rigid motion: the letter turns and nothing about its shape changes. A shear keeps
areas but not shapes: the letter leans and its angles change. The gallery of maps later in this
unit looks at what each kind of map keeps and loses.
