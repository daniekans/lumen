Spans are lines and planes through the origin. Most lines and planes miss the origin, and they
are described the same way, with a starting point added: one direction for a line, two for a
plane. A plane also has a single equation, written with the dot product.

## Lines

The <Term id="parametric-line">line</Term> through the point $\mathbf{p}$ with
direction <C c="blue">$\mathbf{v} \ne \mathbf{0}$</C> is the set of points

$$
\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{p} + t\mathbf{v}, \qquad t \in \mathbb{R}.
$$

Each value of the parameter $t$ gives <StepLink step="line">one point</StepLink>: $t = 0$ is
$\mathbf{p}$, $t = 1$ is one step of $\mathbf{v}$ further. To test whether a point is on the
line, solve for $t$ with one component and check the others. For the line
$(2, -1, 0) + t(-1, 2, 1)$ and the point $(0, 3, 2)$, the first component gives $2 - t = 0$, so
$t = 2$, and the other two agree: $-1 + 4 = 3$, $0 + 2 = 2$.

With $\mathbf{p} = \mathbf{0}$ the line is the <Term id="span">span</Term> of $\mathbf{v}$. Any
other line is that span <StepLink step="shifted-span">moved by</StepLink> $\mathbf{p}$. The
description is not unique: any point of the line can be the starting point, and any nonzero
multiple of $\mathbf{v}$ can be the direction.

Read $t$ as time and the line is motion at constant velocity. A drone that starts at
$\mathbf{p} = (2, -1, 0)$ (in km) and flies with velocity $\mathbf{v} = (-1, 2, 1)$ (in km per
minute) is at $\mathbf{p} + t\mathbf{v}$ after $t$ minutes: at $(0, 3, 2)$ after $2$ minutes, the
point tested above. Its speed is $\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert = \sqrt{6} \approx 2.45$ km per minute.

## Planes

With two directions that are not parallel, <C c="blue">$\mathbf{v}$</C>
and <C c="yellow">$\mathbf{w}$</C>,

$$
\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{p} + s\mathbf{v} + t\mathbf{w}, \qquad s, t \in \mathbb{R},
$$

is the plane through $\mathbf{p}$ along them: their span, moved by $\mathbf{p}$. The example
is $(2, 0, 2) + s(2, -1, 0) + t(0, 1, -1)$, whose parameters $s$ and
$t$ <StepLink step="plane">reach</StepLink> every point of the plane.

## The normal form

A vector $\mathbf{n} \ne \mathbf{0}$ that is <Term id="orthogonal">orthogonal</Term> to both
directions is a <Term id="normal-vector">normal vector</Term> of the plane.
Here <C c="orange">$\mathbf{n} = (1, 2, 2)$</C>: it has dot product $2 - 2 = 0$ with $(2, -1, 0)$ and with $(0, 1, -1)$.
Then $\mathbf{n}$ is orthogonal to every combination $s\mathbf{v} + t\mathbf{w}$, since
$\mathbf{n} \cdot (s\mathbf{v} + t\mathbf{w}) = s\,(\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{v}) + t\,(\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{w}) = 0$.
Those combinations are exactly the arrows $\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{p}$ from $\mathbf{p}$ to points of
the plane, so every point $\mathbf{x}$ of the plane satisfies

$$
\mathbf{n} \cdot (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{p}) = 0.
$$

In the visualization, <StepLink step="normal">drag</StepLink> $\mathbf{x}$ to see the dot product
stay $0$.

The converse holds as well: a point that satisfies the equation is on the plane. Suppose
$\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{p}$ has $\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{u} = 0$ but is not in the span
of $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{w}$. Then $\mathbf{v}$, $\mathbf{w}$, $\mathbf{u}$ do not lie in one
plane through the origin, so they span all of space (Span and independence), and
$\mathbf{n} = a\mathbf{v} + b\mathbf{w} + c\mathbf{u}$ for some weights. Dotting with $\mathbf{n}$,

$$
\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{n} = a\,(\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{v}) + b\,(\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{w}) + c\,(\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{u}) = 0,
$$

so $\lVert \mathbf{n} \rVert = 0$ and $\mathbf{n} = \mathbf{0}$, which a normal vector is not. So
$\mathbf{u}$ is in the span and $\mathbf{x}$ is on the plane: the equation describes the plane
exactly.

## One equation

Writing $\mathbf{x} = (x, y, z)$ and expanding the dot product turns the normal form into a linear
equation:

$$
n_1 x + n_2 y + n_3 z = d, \qquad d = \mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{p}.
$$

For the example, $d = 1 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot 0 + 2 \cdot 2 = 6$, and the plane is
$x + 2y + 2z = 6$. The coefficients are the normal, read straight from
the <StepLink step="equation">equation</StepLink>: the plane $3x - y + z = 4$ has normal $(3, -1, 1)$.
To go back from the equation to a parametric form, find one point that satisfies it and two
directions orthogonal to $\mathbf{n}$ that are not parallel.

In the plane $\mathbb{R}^2$ the same equation $n_1 x + n_2 y = d$ is a line, with $\mathbf{n}$
perpendicular to it. In space one equation gives a plane, so a line in space needs either the
parametric form or two equations (two planes that meet in it).

## A decision boundary

The exam model of the dot product item scores a student $\mathbf{x} = (\text{hours studied},
\text{hours slept})$ with $s = \mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x} + b$, where $\mathbf{w} = (0.5, 0.3)$ and
$b = -4$, and predicts a pass when $s > 0$. The students with score exactly $0$ satisfy

$$
0.5x + 0.3y = 4,
$$

a line with normal $\mathbf{w}$. It splits the plane in two: on the side $\mathbf{w}$ points to the
score is positive, on the other side negative. With $n$ features the boundary
$\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x} + b = 0$ is the same equation in $\mathbb{R}^n$, a flat set called a
hyperplane, and the weights of a linear classifier are its normal vector. Logistic regression and
support vector machines, in Machine Learning, draw boundaries of this kind.

## Planes through the origin

The plane $x + 2y + 2z = d$ contains $\mathbf{0}$ exactly when $d = 0$. Changing
$d$ <StepLink step="through-origin">slides the plane</StepLink> along $\mathbf{n}$ without turning it,
and at $d = 0$ it is the span of $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{w}$. So the spans of two independent
vectors in space are the planes $n_1 x + n_2 y + n_3 z = 0$, and the spans of one nonzero vector are
the lines through the origin. Solution sets of equations with right side $0$ return in the unit
Systems and inverses as null spaces.
