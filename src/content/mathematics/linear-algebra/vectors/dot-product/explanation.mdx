Two vectors can point the same way, opposite ways, or anywhere in between. The dot product
measures this with one number, computed from the components alone, and it is where lengths and
angles come from in every dimension.

## Multiply and add

The <Term id="dot-product">dot product</Term> of two vectors with $n$ components is

$$
\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w} = v_1 w_1 + v_2 w_2 + \cdots + v_n w_n.
$$

For <C c="blue">$\mathbf{v} = (3, 1)$</C> and <C c="yellow">$\mathbf{w} = (1, 2)$</C>
it is $3 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 2 = 5$. The result is a number (a scalar), not a vector. From the
definition, for any vectors and any number $c$:

$$
\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{v}, \qquad
\mathbf{u} \cdot (\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{w}, \qquad
(c\mathbf{v}) \cdot \mathbf{w} = c\,(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w}),
$$

and a vector dotted with itself is the sum of its squared components, its
squared <Term id="vector-length">length</Term>:

$$
\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert^2.
$$

## Shadow times length

In the plane and in space the same number has a picture. Drop a perpendicular from the tip of
$\mathbf{w}$ to the line through $\mathbf{v}$. The point where it lands marks
the <C c="teal">shadow</C> of $\mathbf{w}$ on that line, and its signed length (<C c="red">negative</C> when it
points away from $\mathbf{v}$) is the <Term id="scalar-projection">scalar projection</Term> of
$\mathbf{w}$ on $\mathbf{v}$. <StepLink step="projection">Then</StepLink>

$$
\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w} = \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert \times (\text{shadow of } \mathbf{w}).
$$

The shadow itself, as a vector, is $\frac{\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w}}{\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{v}}\,\mathbf{v}$:
the multiple of $\mathbf{v}$ with that signed length. Projections return in the unit
Orthogonality, where they become the core of least squares.

## The angle

With $\theta$ the angle between the two arrows ($0^\circ \le \theta \le 180^\circ$), the shadow of
$\mathbf{w}$ is $\lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert \cos\theta$, so the shadow formula reads

$$
\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w} = \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert \, \lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert \cos\theta.
$$

Why the component formula and this one agree: the arrows $\mathbf{v}$, $\mathbf{w}$ and
$\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{v}$ form a triangle, and the law of cosines (the Pythagorean theorem with a
correction for angles other than $90^\circ$) says

$$
\lVert \mathbf{w} - \mathbf{v} \rVert^2 = \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert^2 + \lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert^2 - 2\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert \, \lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert \cos\theta.
$$

Expanding the left side with the rules above gives
$(\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{v}) \cdot (\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{v}) = \lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert^2 - 2\,\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w} + \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert^2$.
Comparing the two leaves $\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w} = \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert \lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert \cos\theta$.
The sign of the dot product is the sign of $\cos\theta$: positive
for angles <StepLink step="angle">below</StepLink> $90^\circ$, negative above.

## Orthogonal vectors

At $90^\circ$ the cosine is $0$. Vectors with $\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w} = 0$ are
called <Term id="orthogonal">orthogonal</Term>; for nonzero vectors in the plane or in space this
means <StepLink step="orthogonal">perpendicular</StepLink>. The test needs no angle:
$(3, 1) \cdot (-1, 3) = -3 + 3 = 0$. The zero vector is orthogonal to every vector.

## Lengths bound the dot product

Since $\lvert \cos\theta \rvert \le 1$, in the plane and in space

$$
\lvert \mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w} \rvert \le \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert \, \lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert.
$$

This is the <Term id="cauchy-schwarz">Cauchy-Schwarz inequality</Term>, and it holds in every
$\mathbb{R}^n$, where there is no picture to read an angle from. A proof that uses only the rules
of the dot product: if $\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{0}$ both sides are $0$. Otherwise, for every number
$t$,

$$
0 \le \lVert \mathbf{v} - t\mathbf{w} \rVert^2 = \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert^2 - 2t\,(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w}) + t^2 \lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert^2.
$$

Choosing $t = \frac{\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w}}{\lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert^2}$ gives
$0 \le \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert^2 - \frac{(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w})^2}{\lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert^2}$,
which rearranges to $(\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w})^2 \le \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert^2 \lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert^2$.
Equality needs $\mathbf{v} - t\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{0}$, so it
holds <StepLink step="bound">exactly when</StepLink> one vector is a multiple of the other.

Cauchy-Schwarz gives angles in $\mathbb{R}^n$. For nonzero vectors the ratio
$\frac{\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w}}{\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert \lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert}$ lies
between $-1$ and $1$, so it is the cosine of exactly one angle between $0^\circ$ and $180^\circ$.
That angle is the definition of the angle between vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$, and in the plane and
space it agrees with the one measured on the arrows.

It also proves the <Term id="triangle-inequality">triangle inequality</Term> from vector algebra
in every $\mathbb{R}^n$:

$$
\lVert \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w} \rVert^2 = \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert^2 + 2\,\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w} + \lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert^2
\le \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert^2 + 2\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert \lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert + \lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert^2
= \left(\lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert + \lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert\right)^2.
$$

The middle step uses Cauchy-Schwarz, and taking square roots finishes the proof.
Equality needs $\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w} = \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert \lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert$,
which happens exactly when one vector is a nonnegative multiple of the other.

## Cosine similarity

Text, images and users are often compared as feature vectors: counts of words, pixel values, or
learned embeddings with hundreds of components. Dividing the dot product by both lengths leaves
the cosine of the angle,

$$
\cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{q} \cdot \mathbf{d}}{\lVert \mathbf{q} \rVert \, \lVert \mathbf{d} \rVert},
$$

the <Term id="cosine-similarity">cosine similarity</Term>. It is $1$ for vectors pointing the same
way, $0$ for orthogonal ones, and it ignores length. In
the <StepLink step="similarity">example</StepLink>, a long and a short sports article have counts
$(8, 2)$ and $(3, 1)$ of the words "match" and "election". Their dot product is $26$ and their
lengths are $\sqrt{68}$ and $\sqrt{10}$, so the similarity is $\frac{26}{\sqrt{680}} \approx 0.997$:
the same topic, at different lengths. Search over embeddings ranks documents this way.
