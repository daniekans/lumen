A few vectors and every way of scaling and adding them: which points of space can be reached?
The answer is a line, a plane or all of space, and which one depends on whether some vector
repeats what the others already reach.

## Span

The <Term id="span">span</Term> of vectors $\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_k$ is the set of all
their <Term id="linear-combination">linear combinations</Term>:

$$
\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_k\} = \{\, c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + \cdots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k \;:\; c_1, \ldots, c_k \in \mathbb{R} \,\}.
$$

Taking every weight $0$ shows that a span always contains $\mathbf{0}$, so it passes through the
origin. In space:

- One nonzero vector <C c="blue">$\mathbf{v}$</C> spans the
  <StepLink step="one">line</StepLink> through the origin along it: its multiples $a\mathbf{v}$.
- Two vectors that are not parallel, <C c="blue">$\mathbf{v} = (1, 2, 0)$</C>
  and <C c="yellow">$\mathbf{w} = (-1, 0, 2)$</C>, span a
  <StepLink step="two">plane</StepLink> through the origin. Whole-number weights draw a grid on it,
  as they did in the plane.

## A vector that adds nothing

Take <C c="fg">$\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w} = (0, 2, 2)$</C>. It
lies <StepLink step="in-plane">in the plane</StepLink> of $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{w}$, and any
combination of all three is already a combination of the first two:

$$
a\mathbf{v} + b\mathbf{w} + c\mathbf{u} = (a + c)\mathbf{v} + (b + c)\mathbf{w}.
$$

So $\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w}, \mathbf{u}\}$ is still the plane. Whether a
vector is in a span is a question about weights. For $(0, 2, 2)$, matching components in
$a(1, 2, 0) + b(-1, 0, 2) = (0, 2, 2)$ gives

$$
a - b = 0, \qquad 2a = 2, \qquad 2b = 2,
$$

solved by $a = b = 1$. For $(2, -1, 2)$ the last two equations force $a = -\frac{1}{2}$ and
$b = 1$, and then $a - b = -\frac{3}{2} \ne 2$: there are no weights, and the vector is off the
plane. Three equations in two unknowns usually have no solution, which is why most vectors of
space are outside a given plane.

## Dependence and independence

Vectors $\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_k$ are <Term id="linear-dependence">linearly dependent</Term>
when some combination with weights not all zero gives the zero vector:

$$
c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + \cdots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k = \mathbf{0}, \quad \text{not all } c_i = 0.
$$

Otherwise they are <Term id="linear-independence">linearly independent</Term>: only
$c_1 = \cdots = c_k = 0$ gives $\mathbf{0}$. In the example the combination
$\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w} - \mathbf{u}$ is $\mathbf{0}$, and the three arrows <StepLink step="redundant">close a loop</StepLink> tip to tail.

Dependence means exactly that one vector is a combination of the others. If $c_j \ne 0$ in a
combination equal to $\mathbf{0}$, divide by $c_j$ and solve for $\mathbf{v}_j$; it is a
combination of the rest, and removing it leaves the span unchanged. Conversely, if
$\mathbf{v}_j = \sum_{i \ne j} a_i\mathbf{v}_i$, moving $\mathbf{v}_j$ to the other side gives a
combination equal to $\mathbf{0}$ whose weight on $\mathbf{v}_j$ is $-1$. Two special cases:

- Two vectors are dependent exactly when one is a multiple of the other (they are parallel, or
  one is $\mathbf{0}$).
- Any set that contains $\mathbf{0}$ is dependent, since $1 \cdot \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}$.

## Three vectors in space

Lift $\mathbf{u}$ off the plane, to <C c="fg">$(2, -1, 2)$</C>. Each value of $c$ gives a copy of
the plane moved by $c\mathbf{u}$, and these copies <StepLink step="space">fill space</StepLink>,
so the span is all of $\mathbb{R}^3$. Now no loop closes: three vectors of space are independent
exactly when they do not lie in one plane through the origin.

Independence also makes the weights unique. If two combinations give the same vector,
$\sum a_i\mathbf{v}_i = \sum b_i\mathbf{v}_i$, then $\sum (a_i - b_i)\mathbf{v}_i = \mathbf{0}$, and
independence forces $a_i = b_i$ for every $i$. Independent vectors that span a space form a
basis, and their unique weights are coordinates (Basis and coordinates, later in this unit). A fourth vector in space is
always a combination of three independent ones, so four vectors of $\mathbb{R}^3$ are always
dependent; the unit Vector spaces and change of basis proves the general fact, that more than $n$
vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ are dependent.
