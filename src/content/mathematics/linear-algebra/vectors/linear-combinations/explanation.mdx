Adding and scaling are the two operations on vectors. Used together they give the central
expression of linear algebra: which points can be reached by scaling a few fixed vectors and
adding the results?

## Linear combinations

For vectors $\mathbf{v}$, $\mathbf{w}$ and numbers $a$, $b$, the vector

$$
a\mathbf{v} + b\mathbf{w}
$$

is a <Term id="linear-combination">linear combination</Term> of $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{w}$
with weights $a$ and $b$. With more vectors it is $c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \cdots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k$.
In the picture, <C c="blue">$a\mathbf{v}$</C> and <C c="yellow">$b\mathbf{w}$</C> are placed tip to
tail, and the <C c="teal">combination</C> is the arrow to the end. In components, with
$\mathbf{v} = (2, 1)$ and $\mathbf{w} = (-1, 2)$:

$$
a\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + b\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} 2a - b \\ a + 2b \end{bmatrix}.
$$

## Finding the weights

To <StepLink step="target">reach</StepLink> $\mathbf{u} = (3, 4)$, each component must match:

$$
\begin{aligned}
2a - b &= 3, \\
a + 2b &= 4.
\end{aligned}
$$

Doubling the first equation and adding the second gives $5a = 10$, so $a = 2$ and $b = 1$. A
question about vectors became two equations in two unknowns. Systems of equations come back in
the unit Systems and inverses, where this observation is the starting point.

## A grid of its own

Whole-number weights mark a <StepLink step="grid">slanted grid</StepLink>: the lines where $a$ is
fixed run along $\mathbf{w}$, and the lines where $b$ is fixed run along $\mathbf{v}$. When
$\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{w}$ are not parallel, every point of the plane has exactly one pair of
weights, whole or not, just as it has one pair of coordinates. The reason is the same elimination
as above, done for any target $(x, y)$:

$$
a = \frac{2x + y}{5}, \qquad b = \frac{2y - x}{5}.
$$

Every target gives one answer. The $5$ is $v_1 w_2 - v_2 w_1 = 2 \cdot 2 - 1 \cdot (-1)$, and the
same elimination for any two vectors divides by that number. It is $0$ exactly when one vector is a
multiple of the other, the case below. The number comes back as the determinant in the unit
Determinants.

The weights are coordinates measured with $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{w}$ instead of the usual unit
steps; the item on bases makes this precise.

## When the vectors are parallel

If $\mathbf{w}$ is a multiple of $\mathbf{v}$, say $\mathbf{w} = -\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{v}$, then

$$
a\mathbf{v} + b\mathbf{w} = \left(a - \tfrac{b}{2}\right)\mathbf{v},
$$

a multiple of $\mathbf{v}$ for every choice of weights. The
combinations <StepLink step="parallel">fill only a line</StepLink>, points off it are out of reach, and each
point on it is reached by infinitely many pairs $(a, b)$. Whether a set of vectors reaches a whole
plane, a line or all of space is the question of span, which the unit takes up again in 3D.

## Mixing two foods

Linear combinations appear wherever amounts add up. Write a food as the vector of what one serving
contains, in grams of protein and carbs. With <C c="blue">food A</C> $= (20, 10)$ and the second
food, <C c="yellow">food B</C> $= (5, 20)$, a meal of $a$ servings of A and $b$ servings of B
contains

$$
a\begin{bmatrix} 20 \\ 10 \end{bmatrix} + b\begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 20 \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} 20a + 5b \\ 10a + 20b \end{bmatrix}.
$$

A meal with $35$ g of each needs $20a + 5b = 35$ and $10a + 20b = 35$, which
gives <StepLink step="mix">$a = 1.5$ and $b = 1$</StepLink>. The units explain why this works:
grams per serving times servings gives grams, and grams add.

Here the weights cannot be negative. With $a, b \ge 0$ the meals do not fill the plane, only
the <C c="teal">wedge</C> between the two arrows, a cone. A meal with $5$ g of protein and $40$ g
of carbs is outside it: the equations give $a = -\frac{2}{7}$, a negative amount of food A. Limits
on the weights like this one are the starting point of linear programming, which plans diets,
production and shipping.
