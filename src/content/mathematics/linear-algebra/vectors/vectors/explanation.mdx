Linear algebra is about quantities that need more than one number: a position in the plane, a
velocity, a pixel's color, a row of a spreadsheet. A <Term id="vector">vector</Term> packs those
numbers into one object, and the same object can be drawn as an arrow.

## Arrows and lists

The vector $\mathbf{v} = (3, 1)$ is the list of its components $3$ and $1$, and also the arrow
that goes $3$ to the right and $1$ up. Drawn from the origin, it ends at the point $(3, 1)$. In
print vectors are bold ($\mathbf{v}$); by hand they get an arrow ($\vec{v}$). They are often
written as a column:

$$
\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

An arrow in $\mathbb{R}^2$ or $\mathbb{R}^3$ is fixed by its length and its direction, not by where
it starts. <StepLink step="same-vector">Moving it</StepLink> without turning or stretching it gives
the same vector: what matters is tip minus tail. (The zero vector, which comes up
below, is the one exception: length $0$ and no direction.)

The set of all vectors with $n$ real components is <Term id="real-coordinate-space">$\mathbb{R}^n$</Term>.
$\mathbb{R}^2$ is the plane and $\mathbb{R}^3$ is space. For $n \ge 4$ there is no picture, but
the lists and their rules work exactly the same. Later units (Vector spaces) also call other objects
vectors, such as polynomials and functions, because they add and scale by the same rules; the lists
of this unit stay vectors, as one example among those.

## Addition

Vectors add <C c="blue">tip</C> to <C c="yellow">tail</C>: walk along $\mathbf{v}$, then along
$\mathbf{w}$. The horizontal steps add and the vertical steps add, so in components

$$
\begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} w_1 \\ w_2 \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} v_1 + w_1 \\ v_2 + w_2 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Walking in the <StepLink step="order">other order</StepLink> traces the other two sides of a
parallelogram and ends at the same place, so $\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{w} + \mathbf{v}$.

## Scaling

A number $c$ is called a <Term id="scalar">scalar</Term> here, because it scales:

$$
c\begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} cv_1 \\ cv_2 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

The arrow $c\mathbf{v}$ lies on the line through $\mathbf{v}$, is $\lvert c \rvert$ times as long,
and points the other way when $c < 0$. With $c = 0$ every component is $0$:
the <Term id="zero-vector">zero vector</Term> $\mathbf{0}$, which has no direction.

## Subtraction

$\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{v}$ means $\mathbf{w} + (-1)\mathbf{v}$. As an arrow it
goes <StepLink step="subtract">from the tip of $\mathbf{v}$ to the tip of $\mathbf{w}$</StepLink>,
since adding it to $\mathbf{v}$ must give $\mathbf{w}$. It is the vector that answers "how do I
get from here to there".

## Data as vectors

A table with numeric columns is a stack of vectors: each <StepLink step="table">row</StepLink> is
one point in $\mathbb{R}^n$, with one component per column. Machine learning calls such a row a
feature vector. Operations on rows are vector operations. The average row, for example, is

$$
\bar{\mathbf{r}} = \tfrac{1}{3}(\mathbf{r}_1 + \mathbf{r}_2 + \mathbf{r}_3) = \tfrac{1}{3}(15, 21) = (5, 7).
$$

Adding and scaling are the only two operations so far. Combining them is the subject of the next
item, linear combinations.
