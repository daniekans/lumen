The determinant is the factor by which a map scales areas or volumes. Doing one map after
another scales twice, so the factors multiply. This item proves that for every size of matrix and
follows it to powers, undoing a map, scaling a matrix, a change of basis and a first look at
eigenvalues.

## The product rule

$$
\det(AB) = \det A \det B \qquad \text{for square } A, B \text{ of the same size.}
$$

In the plane it is the <StepLink step="twice">picture</StepLink>: $BA$ first applies $A$, which
multiplies every area by $\det A$, then $B$, which multiplies every area, the new ones included, by
$\det B$. A photo enlarged to $2$ times its width and height has $4$ times the pixels; enlarged again
by $1.5$ in each direction, it has $4 \times 2.25 = 9$ times the original, the factor of enlarging
by $3$ at once.

**Proof for every $n$, from the rules.** Fix $B$.

- If $\det B \ne 0$, look at $D(A) = \frac{\det(BA)}{\det B}$ as a function of the columns of $A$.
  The columns of $BA$ are $B\mathbf{a}_1, \ldots, B\mathbf{a}_n$, and $B$ is linear, so $D$ is linear
  in each column. Two equal columns of $A$ give two equal columns of $BA$, so $D = 0$. And
  $D(I) = \frac{\det B}{\det B} = 1$. $D$ obeys the three rules, which fix the determinant, so
  $D(A) = \det A$.
- If $\det B = 0$, the columns of $B$ are dependent (previous items), so $B$ collapses space onto a
  lower dimension. Every column of $BA$ is $B$ times something, so it lies in that smaller image,
  and $n$ vectors in fewer than $n$ dimensions are dependent. So $\det(BA) = 0 = \det A \det B$.

The order of the factors does not matter for the number, $\det(AB) = \det(BA)$, even though
$AB \ne BA$ in general.

**Signs.** A <StepLink step="flips">reflection</StepLink> has $\det = -1$. Two reflections give
$(-1)(-1) = 1$: the orientation is flipped twice, so it is kept, and the result is a rotation. In
general, $BA$ keeps orientation when $A$ and $B$ both keep it or both flip it.

## Powers

$A^k$ is $A$ applied $k$ times, so <StepLink step="powers">$\det(A^k) = (\det A)^k$</StepLink>. With
$\det A = 1.5$, ten applications scale areas by $1.5^{10} \approx 57.7$. A map with
$|\det A| < 1$ shrinks areas toward $0$ as it is repeated.

## Undoing a map

Suppose a map $B$ undoes $A$: $BA = I$. The product rule gives

$$
\det B \det A = \det I = 1, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \det A \ne 0 \quad\text{and}\quad \det B = \frac{1}{\det A}.
$$

In <StepLink step="inverse">the picture</StepLink>, $A$ multiplies areas by $\det A$, and the map
back must divide them by the same factor. When $\det A = 0$ the plane is squeezed onto a line (or a
point): many points land on the same image, and no map can send them back to where they came from.
So $\det A \ne 0$ is necessary for $A$ to have an inverse $A^{-1}$. That it is also enough, and how
to find $A^{-1}$, is the subject of Systems and inverses.

## Scaling the whole matrix

$cA$ multiplies every column by $c$. Rule 2 takes the factor $c$ out of each of the $n$ columns:

$$
\det(cA) = c^n \det A.
$$

In <StepLink step="scalar">the plane</StepLink>, $c = 3$ fits $9$ copies of the parallelogram inside
the new one; in space $c = 2$ multiplies volumes by $8$. A negative $c$ flips orientation in odd
dimensions only: $\det(-A) = (-1)^n\det A$, so $-I$ in the plane is a rotation by $180^\circ$, while
in space it is a point reflection.

## The same in every basis

The determinant belongs to the map, not to the grid used to write it. In a basis
$\mathbf{b}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{b}_n$, collected as the columns of $P$, the same map has the matrix
$P^{-1}AP$ (Vector spaces builds this). The product rule gives

$$
\det(P^{-1}AP) = \det P^{-1} \det A \det P = \frac{1}{\det P}\det A \det P = \det A.
$$

In <StepLink step="basis">the picture</StepLink>, the cell of any basis is sent to a cell with $\det
A$ times its area, so the ratio is the same whichever basis is chosen.

## A preview: when does $A - \lambda I$ collapse?

$A - \lambda I$ subtracts $\lambda$ from the diagonal. For
the <StepLink step="eigen">matrix of the last step</StepLink>, $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end
{bmatrix}$,

$$
\det(A - \lambda I) = (2 - \lambda)^2 - 1 = (\lambda - 1)(\lambda - 3),
$$

which is $0$ exactly at $\lambda = 1$ and $\lambda = 3$. For those values $A - \lambda I$ collapses
the plane onto a line, so some nonzero $\mathbf{v}$ has $(A - \lambda I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$,
that is $A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}$: a direction that $A$ only stretches. These $\lambda$ are
the eigenvalues, and finding them through $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$ is the opening of the
Eigenvalues unit.

## In machine learning

- **Log-determinants add.** A deep model that applies invertible maps $A_1, \ldots, A_L$ in a row
  changes volumes by $\det(A_L \cdots A_1) = \det A_L \cdots \det A_1$. The product of many factors
  overflows or underflows, so code adds logarithms instead:
  $\log|\det(A_L \cdots A_1)| = \sum_k \log|\det A_k|$. Normalizing flows, which turn a simple
  distribution into a complex one by such maps, keep track of probability densities this way, and
  the density of a multivariate Gaussian (in the Probability course) carries $\det\Sigma$ in the
  same role.
- **Dependent features.** For a feature table $X$ (one row per example, one column per feature),
  the Gram matrix $X^TX$ has $\det(X^TX) = 0$ exactly when the features are dependent. If
  $X\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ for some $\mathbf{v} \ne \mathbf{0}$, then $X^TX\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$
  and $X^TX$ collapses. Conversely, if $X^TX\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$, then
  $\lVert X\mathbf{v} \rVert^2 = \mathbf{v}^TX^TX\mathbf{v} = 0$, so $X\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$.
  This is the multicollinearity of the linear maps unit, measured by one number; in floating point,
  check the rank or the condition number rather than $\det$ (see computing determinants).
