The determinant so far is a picture: the signed area of the parallelogram of the columns, or the
signed volume of the box. This item finds three properties of that number which pin it down
completely. Every way of computing a determinant, and every fact about it in this unit, follows
from them, and they make sense for square matrices of any size, where no picture is available.

## The three rules

Write $\det[\mathbf{a}_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf{a}_n]$ for the determinant of the matrix with columns
$\mathbf{a}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{a}_n$.

1. **The identity** has $\det I = 1$.
2. **Linear in each column.** With the other columns fixed, scaling one column scales the
   determinant, and a sum in one column splits into a sum of determinants.
3. **Two equal columns** give $\det = 0$.

For two columns, rule 2 in the first column reads

$$
\det[c\mathbf{u}\ \ \mathbf{w}] = c\det[\mathbf{u}\ \mathbf{w}], \qquad
\det[\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}\ \ \mathbf{w}] = \det[\mathbf{u}\ \mathbf{w}] + \det[\mathbf{v}\ \mathbf{w}],
$$

and the same holds in the second column, or in any column of a larger matrix.

Rule 2 is about one column at a time, not the whole matrix. Scaling the whole matrix scales every
column, so $\det(cA) = c^n\det A$ for an $n \times n$ matrix: doubling a $2 \times 2$ matrix
multiplies areas by $4$, not $2$. And $\det(A + B)$ is in general not $\det A + \det B$: with
$A = B = I$ in the plane, $\det(2I) = 4$ while $\det I + \det I = 2$.

## Why the signed area obeys them

For <StepLink step="identity">rule 1</StepLink>, the identity sends the unit square to itself, area
$1$, turning counterclockwise from $\hat{\imath}$ to $\hat{\jmath}$.

For rule 2, take the second column $\mathbf{w}$ as the base. The area of the parallelogram is
the length of $\mathbf{w}$ times the height of $\mathbf{u}$ above the line of $\mathbf{w}$. Let
$\mathbf{n}$ be the <Term id="unit-vector">unit vector</Term> a quarter turn clockwise from
$\mathbf{w}$, <Term id="orthogonal">perpendicular</Term> to it, and count the height as
$\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{u}$: positive when $\mathbf{u}$ is on the side of $\mathbf{n}$, which
is exactly when the turn from $\mathbf{u}$ to $\mathbf{w}$ is counterclockwise. So the signed area
is $\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert\,(\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{u})$, and a dot product with a fixed vector
is linear in $\mathbf{u}$. So <StepLink step="scale">scaling $\mathbf{u}$ by $c$</StepLink>
scales the height and the determinant by $c$ (a negative $c$ moves $\mathbf{u}$ to the other side
and flips the sign), and <StepLink step="add">the heights of $\mathbf{u}$ and
$\mathbf{v}$ add</StepLink> to the height of $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}$. In the picture the
parallelogram of $\mathbf{u}$ and the one of $\mathbf{v}$, stacked on the same base, have the
same total area as the one of $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}$. With the first column as the base, the
same argument works for the second column. In space the base is the parallelogram of two columns
and the height is that of the third, as in the previous item.

Rule 3: <StepLink step="equal">two equal columns</StepLink> span a flat parallelogram, area $0$.

## What follows from the rules

From here on only the rules are used, never the picture, so everything holds for every $n$.

**A shear keeps the determinant.** <StepLink step="shear">Adding a multiple</StepLink> of one
column to another:

$$
\det[\mathbf{u} + k\mathbf{w}\ \ \mathbf{w}] = \det[\mathbf{u}\ \mathbf{w}] + k\det[\mathbf{w}\ \mathbf{w}] = \det[\mathbf{u}\ \mathbf{w}].
$$

In the picture, $\mathbf{u}$ slides parallel to the base, so the height stays.

**A swap flips the sign.** Put $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{w}$ in both columns. Rule 3 gives $0$, and
rule 2, used in each column, splits it into four terms, two of them with equal columns:

$$
0 = \det[\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{w}\ \ \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{w}]
= \det[\mathbf{u}\ \mathbf{u}] + \det[\mathbf{u}\ \mathbf{w}] + \det[\mathbf{w}\ \mathbf{u}] + \det[\mathbf{w}\ \mathbf{w}]
= \det[\mathbf{u}\ \mathbf{w}] + \det[\mathbf{w}\ \mathbf{u}].
$$

So $\det[\mathbf{w}\ \mathbf{u}] = -\det[\mathbf{u}\ \mathbf{w}]$.
The <StepLink step="swap">swap</StepLink> reverses the turn from the first column to the second.

**Dependent columns give $0$.** If one column is
a <Term id="linear-combination">combination</Term> of the others, say $\mathbf{a}_1 = c_2\mathbf{a}_2 + \cdots + c_n\mathbf{a}_n$,
rule 2 splits the determinant into terms $c_j\det[\mathbf{a}_j\ \mathbf{a}_2\ \cdots\ \mathbf{a}_n]$,
and each has the column $\mathbf{a}_j$ twice, so each is $0$. In the
plane: <StepLink step="dependent">$\det[\mathbf{u}\ \ k\mathbf{u}] = k\det[\mathbf{u}\ \mathbf{u}] = 0$</StepLink>.

## The rules fix the number

Shears and swaps can turn any square matrix into a triangular one. In
the <StepLink step="triangular">last step</StepLink>,

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}
\xrightarrow{\ \mathbf{a}_2 - \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{a}_1\ }
\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 1.5 \end{bmatrix}
\xrightarrow{\ \mathbf{a}_1 - \frac{2}{3}\mathbf{a}_2\ }
\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1.5 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Both shears keep the determinant, and the last matrix has columns $2\hat{\imath}$ and
$1.5\hat{\jmath}$, so rule 2 (twice) and rule 1 give $\det = 2 \times 1.5 \times \det I = 3$.

The same works for every $n$. Go through the rows from the top. In row $i$, pick a column among
the $i$-th and later ones with a nonzero entry in that row and swap it into place $i$ (each swap
flips the sign); then subtract multiples of it from the later columns to clear their entries in
row $i$ (shears). The result is triangular, with zeros above the diagonal. If at some row no such
column exists, the diagonal entry there is $0$. Otherwise the diagonal entries $p_1, \ldots, p_n$
are nonzero, more shears (multiples of each column subtracted from the earlier ones, starting
from the last column) clear the entries below the diagonal without changing it, and rules 2 and
1 give

$$
\det A = \pm\, p_1 p_2 \cdots p_n,
$$

with the sign set by the number of swaps. A triangular matrix with a $0$ on the diagonal has
dependent columns: if the $0$ is in column $j$, the columns $j$ to $n$ have zeros in rows $1$ to
$j$, so they are $n - j + 1$ vectors in a space of $n - j$ dimensions. Then its determinant is $0$.

Every step used only the rules, so any function with the three properties gives the same
number: the rules fix the determinant. The signed area and the signed volume have the properties,
so they are this function. In higher dimensions the rules are the definition, and the lesson on
computing determinants shows that such a function exists for every $n$ (the cofactor formula).

The reduction also gives the converse of the last section. Shears and swaps can be undone, so
they keep columns independent or dependent. If the columns of $A$
are <Term id="linear-independence">independent</Term>, the triangular result has independent columns,
so no $0$ on its diagonal, and $\det A \ne 0$. Together:

$$
\det A = 0 \quad\text{exactly when the columns of } A \text{ are dependent},
$$

that is, exactly when the map collapses space onto a lower dimension.
