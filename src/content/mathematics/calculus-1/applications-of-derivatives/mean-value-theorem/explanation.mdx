A derivative is a fact about one point. Statements like "$f' > 0$ on an interval, so $f$ is
increasing there" are about the whole interval. The mean value theorem connects the two.

## Rolle's theorem

If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, differentiable on $(a, b)$, and $f(a) = f(b)$, then there is a
$c$ in $(a, b)$ with $f'(c) = 0$.

The proof uses the last item. By the <Term id="extreme-value-theorem">extreme value theorem</Term>, $f$ has a maximum and a minimum on
$[a, b]$. If both are at the ends, they are equal (since $f(a) = f(b)$), so $f$ is constant and $f'
= 0$ everywhere inside. Otherwise one of them is at some $c$ inside, where it is a local extremum,
and <Term id="fermats-theorem">Fermat's theorem</Term> gives $f'(c) = 0$.
In <StepLink step="rolle">the first step</StepLink>, $A$ and $B$ are at the same height and the flat
tangent is at the top of the arch.

## The mean value theorem

If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and differentiable on $(a, b)$, then there is a $c$ in $(a, b)$
with

$$
f'(c) = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}.
$$

The right side is the slope of the <C c="orange">chord</C> from $A$ to $B$, the average rate of
change. The theorem says that some <C c="teal">tangent</C> is <StepLink step="mvt">parallel to the chord</StepLink>: at some instant, the rate equals the average
rate. It says that such a $c$ exists, not where it is, and there may be more than one.

**Proof.** <StepLink step="proof">Subtract the chord</StepLink> from the curve:

$$
h(x) = f(x) - f(a) - \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}(x - a).
$$

$h$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and differentiable on $(a, b)$, and $h(a) = h(b) = 0$. Rolle gives
a $c$ with $h'(c) = 0$, that is, $f'(c) - \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a} = 0$. The mean value theorem
is Rolle's theorem with the picture tilted.

## The conditions matter

A <StepLink step="fails">corner</StepLink> breaks the theorem. For $|x - 2| + 1$ on $[0.5, 4]$,
the chord has slope $\frac{3 - 2.5}{3.5} = \frac{1}{7}$, and no tangent has that slope. The
function is continuous, but not differentiable at $2$. A function with a jump fails in the same
way.

## An average speed check

Some highways measure speed over a section: a camera records the time at each end, and the
average speed is the distance over the time. A car that covers 160 km in 1.5 hours averages
$\frac{160}{1.5} \approx 106.7$ km/h. With $s(t)$ the distance driven after $t$ hours, the mean
value theorem gives an instant $c$ with

$$
s'(c) = \frac{s(1.5) - s(0)}{1.5 - 0} \approx 106.7\ \text{km/h}.
$$

So at some moment the <StepLink step="speed">speedometer</StepLink> read the average, and if that
is over the limit, the car was over the limit then. Position is continuous and the speedometer
reading is its derivative, so the conditions hold.

## Consequences

These are the facts the previous item used.

**$f' = 0$ means constant.** If $f'(x) = 0$ for every $x$ in an interval, take any two points
$x_1 < x_2$ in it. The mean value theorem gives $f(x_2) - f(x_1) = f'(c)(x_2 - x_1) = 0$. So all
values are equal.

**$f' > 0$ means increasing.** If $f' > 0$ on an interval, then for $x_1 < x_2$ in it,
$f(x_2) - f(x_1) = f'(c)(x_2 - x_1) > 0$. So $f$ is <Term id="increasing-decreasing">increasing</Term>. With $f' < 0$, decreasing. This is why the <Term id="first-derivative-test">first derivative test</Term> works.

**Same derivative, a constant apart.** If $f' = g'$ on an interval, then $(f - g)' = 0$, so
$f - g = C$ for some constant. Their graphs are <StepLink step="same-slope">vertical shifts</StepLink>
of each other. This will matter for integrals: the functions whose derivative is a given $f$
differ only by a constant, written $+ C$.

All three need an interval. $f(x) = \frac{1}{x}$ has $f'(x) = -\frac{1}{x^2} < 0$ wherever it is
defined, yet $f(-1) = -1 < 1 = f(1)$: the domain is two intervals, and the theorem applies on each
separately.
