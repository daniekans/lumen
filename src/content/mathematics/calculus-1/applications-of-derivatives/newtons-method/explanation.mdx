Most equations have no formula for their roots: $x^3 - 2x - 5 = 0$, $\cos x = x$.
The <Term id="linearization">linear approximation</Term> offers a way around. Near a guess, $f$ is
close to its tangent line, and the root of a line is one division away. Solve the line instead,
and repeat from the answer.

## The step

The tangent at $x_n$ is $L(x) = f(x_n) + f'(x_n)(x - x_n)$. Setting $L(x) = 0$ gives the next
guess:

$$
x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}.
$$

In the language of <Term id="differential">differentials</Term>, the step is the $dx$ whose
$dy = f'(x_n)\,dx$ equals $-f(x_n)$: the change in $x$ that the tangent predicts will bring $f$
down to $0$. The step needs $f'(x_n) \neq 0$. A <StepLink step="tangent-step">flat tangent</StepLink>
never meets the axis, so there is no next guess.

For $f(x) = x^2 - 2$ from $x_0 = 2.6$, the <StepLink step="iterate">iterates</StepLink> are
$1.6846$, $1.4359$, $1.414378$ and $1.41421357$; $\sqrt{2} = 1.41421356\ldots$

## How fast: the error is squared

Let $r$ be a root with $f'(r) \neq 0$ (a simple root), and let $f''$ be continuous near $r$.
Write $e_n = x_n - r$ for the error. A second-order version of the linear approximation, proved in
Calculus 2 with the Taylor remainder, gives a $\xi$ between $x_n$ and $r$ with

$$
0 = f(r) = f(x_n) + f'(x_n)(r - x_n) + \tfrac{1}{2} f''(\xi)(r - x_n)^2.
$$

Divide by $f'(x_n)$ and rearrange, and the first two terms become $-e_{n+1}$:

$$
e_{n+1} = \frac{f''(\xi)}{2f'(x_n)}\,e_n^2.
$$

Near the root the fraction is close to the constant $\frac{f''(r)}{2f'(r)}$, so an error of about
$10^{-k}$ becomes one of about $10^{-2k}$. This
is <Term id="quadratic-convergence">quadratic convergence</Term>: the number
of <StepLink step="digits">correct decimals</StepLink> roughly doubles each step. For $\sqrt{2}$ from
$2.6$ the counts are about $1.7$, $3.8$, $8.0$, $16.5$ and $33.4$. A float holds about 16
significant digits, so the stage stops counting there.

It takes a simple root. At a double root, such as $0$ for $x^2$, the step is
$x - \frac{x^2}{2x} = \frac{x}{2}$: the error only halves, one binary digit per step.

## Square roots, the Babylonian way

For $f(x) = x^2 - a$ the step simplifies:

$$
x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{x_n^2 - a}{2x_n} = \frac{1}{2}\left(x_n + \frac{a}{x_n}\right).
$$

A rectangle with sides $x_n$ and $\frac{a}{x_n}$ has area $a$. If one side is too long, the other
is too short, and their average is a better side for a square.
The <StepLink step="babylonian">rectangles</StepLink> turn into the square of side $\sqrt{a}$. The
method is ancient: it carries the name of Babylonian mathematicians, whose tablets give $\sqrt{2}$
to about six decimal places, and Heron of Alexandria described it in the first century, long
before Newton. It converges from every $x_0 > 0$: the average of two positive
numbers is at least their geometric mean, so $x_1 \ge \sqrt{a}$, and from there the iterates
decrease to $\sqrt{a}$.

Software and hardware often compute square roots and reciprocals this way: a rough first guess
from a small table, then a few Newton steps, each doubling the correct bits.

## When it fails

The fast convergence holds from starts close enough to a simple root, and "close enough" can be
small.

- **A flat tangent.** $f'(x_n) = 0$ stops the method, and a nearly flat one throws the next guess
  far away.
- **A cycle.** For $f(x) = x^3 - 2x + 2$, <StepLink step="cycle">$x_0 = 0$</StepLink> gives
  $x_1 = 0 - \frac{2}{-2} = 1$, and $x_2 = 1 - \frac{1}{1} = 0$. Nearby starts are drawn into the
  same cycle, while $x_0 = -1$ reaches the root $-1.7693$ in a few steps.
- **Divergence.** For $f(x) = \sqrt[3]{x}$, $f'(x) = \frac{1}{3}x^{-2/3}$ and
  <StepLink step="diverge">the step</StepLink> is $x - 3x = -2x$: every start moves away from the
  root. The theorem does not apply, since $f'(0)$ does not exist (the tangent at the root is
  vertical).

In practice Newton's method is guarded: start from a bracket where $f$ changes sign, and fall back
to bisection (from the <Term id="intermediate-value-theorem">intermediate value theorem</Term>)
whenever a step leaves the bracket.

## Newton on the derivative: a first optimizer

A minimum of $g$ is at a <Term id="critical-point">critical point</Term>, a root of $g'$. Newton's
method on $g'$ is

$$
x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{g'(x_n)}{g''(x_n)}.
$$

The picture: at $x_n$, take the parabola with the same value, slope and second derivative,
$q(x) = g(x_n) + g'(x_n)(x - x_n) + \frac{1}{2}g''(x_n)(x - x_n)^2$. Its derivative is $0$ where
$x - x_n = -\frac{g'(x_n)}{g''(x_n)}$, which is the step. So <StepLink step="minimum">each
step</StepLink> jumps to the lowest point of the fitted <C c="teal">parabola</C>. For
$g(x) = \frac{x^4}{4} - x + 2$ from $x_0 = 2.2$, the iterates are $1.536$, $1.165$, $1.022$ and
$1.0005$, approaching the minimum at $1$.

The method finds critical points, not only minima. Where $g'' < 0$ the parabola opens downward and
the step heads for a maximum, so the <Term id="second-derivative-test">second derivative
test</Term> checks the result.

Gradient descent, the optimizer behind neural networks (see the item
[Backpropagation](/machine-learning/neural-networks/training/backpropagation)), takes
$x_{n+1} = x_n - \eta\,g'(x_n)$ with a fixed small rate $\eta$. It trusts only the linear
approximation, so it takes small steps. Newton's method uses the curvature too, in effect choosing
$\eta = \frac{1}{g''(x_n)}$. With many variables $g''$ becomes a matrix of second derivatives (the
Hessian, in Multivariable Calculus), and its size, one entry per pair of parameters, is the reason
large models use gradient descent and its variants instead.
