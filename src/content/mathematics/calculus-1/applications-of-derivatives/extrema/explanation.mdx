Where does a function reach its highest and lowest values? The derivative narrows the search
from every point of the domain to a handful of candidates.

## Local and absolute extrema

$f(c)$ is a local maximum when $f(c) \ge f(x)$ for every $x$ in some open interval around $c$,
and a local minimum when $f(c) \le f(x)$ there. Local means "the best among the neighbors". The <Term id="maximum-minimum">absolute maximum and minimum</Term> are the largest and smallest values
over the whole domain.

For <C c="blue">$f(x) = \frac{1}{4}x^3 - \frac{3}{2}x^2 + \frac{9}{4}x + 1$</C>, <StepLink step="local">$f(1) = 2$</StepLink> is a local maximum and $f(3) = 1$ a local minimum.
Neither is absolute: the graph climbs past $2$ to the right of $x = 4$.

## Fermat's theorem

If $f$ has a local extremum at $c$ and $f'(c)$ exists, then $f'(c) = 0$.

Take a local maximum. For small $h > 0$, $f(c + h) \le f(c)$, so the secant to the right has
slope $\frac{f(c + h) - f(c)}{h} \le 0$. To the left, $f(c - h) \le f(c)$, so
$\frac{f(c) - f(c - h)}{h} \ge 0$. Both quotients tend to $f'(c)$ as <StepLink step="fermat">$h \to 0$</StepLink>, and a limit of numbers $\le 0$ is $\le 0$, a limit of
numbers $\ge 0$ is $\ge 0$. So $f'(c) = 0$. A minimum works the same way.

The condition "$f'(c)$ exists" matters: $|x|$ has a minimum at $0$ with no derivative there.

## Critical points

A critical point of $f$ is a point $c$ of its domain where $f'(c) = 0$ or $f'(c)$ does not exist.
Fermat's theorem says that every local extremum inside the domain happens at a critical point. So
to find extrema, find the critical points first.

The converse is false. <StepLink step="critical">At $x = 2$</StepLink>, $\frac{1}{2}(x - 2)^3 +
1.5$ has a flat tangent and keeps rising on both sides: a critical point that is no extremum. The
corner of $0.8\,|x - 2| + 0.6$ is a critical point because $f'(2)$ does not exist, and it is a
minimum. Critical points are candidates, not answers.

## The first derivative test

Where $f' > 0$ on an interval, $f$ is <Term id="increasing-decreasing">increasing</Term> there; where $f' < 0$, decreasing. The next
item proves this with the mean value theorem; here it is used. Then, at a critical point $c$ of a
continuous $f$:

- $f'$ changes from $+$ to $-$ at $c$: $f(c)$ is a local maximum;
- $f'$ changes from $-$ to $+$: a local minimum;
- $f'$ keeps its sign: neither.

For the example, $f'(x) = \frac{3}{4}(x - 1)(x - 3)$ has the <StepLink step="first-test">signs</StepLink> <C c="green">$+$</C>, <C c="red">$-$</C>, <C c="green">$+$</C> on $(-\infty, 1)$, $(1, 3)$, $(3, \infty)$, so $x = 1$ is a maximum and $x = 3$
a minimum. A sign chart like this needs only the critical points and one test value in each
interval.

## Absolute extrema on a closed interval

The extreme value theorem: a function continuous on a closed, bounded interval $[a, b]$ has an
absolute maximum and an absolute minimum there. Its proof rests on the completeness of the real
numbers (no gaps in the number line) and belongs to a course in analysis.

An absolute extremum is either inside the interval, where it is also a local one and so at a
critical point, or at an end. That gives the closed interval method:

1. Find the critical points of $f$ in $(a, b)$.
2. Evaluate $f$ at them and at $a$ and $b$.
3. The largest value is the absolute maximum, the smallest the absolute minimum.

Now <StepLink step="closed">drag the ends</StepLink>: on $[0.2, 4.4]$ the maximum is at the end $b =
4.4$, not at the local maximum.

## A drug's peak

After a dose, a common model for the concentration in the blood is <C c="blue">$C(t) = 8t\,e^{-t / 2}$</C> mg/L, with $t$ in hours. Its derivative is

$$
C'(t) = 8e^{-t/2} - 4t\,e^{-t/2} = 4e^{-t/2}(2 - t),
$$

zero only at $t = 2$, positive before and negative after. So the <StepLink step="dose">peak</StepLink> is $C(2) = \frac{16}{e} \approx 5.89$ mg/L, two hours after the dose. On $[0, 12]$ the minimum is at the end $t = 0$,
where the concentration is $0$. The units read as in Leibniz notation: $C'(t)$ is in mg/L per hour.

## When there is no maximum

Both conditions of the extreme value theorem matter. On the open interval $(0.5, 4.4)$, $f$ gets
as close to $f(4.4)$ as you like without reaching it, so there is no largest value. On $[0, 4]$, a
function with a <StepLink step="no-max">jump</StepLink> can do the same. The method above
assumes the conditions; check them before using it.
