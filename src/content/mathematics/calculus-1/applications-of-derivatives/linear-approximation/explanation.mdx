A differentiable function looks straight when you zoom in on it. That straight line, the <Term id="tangent-line">tangent</Term>, is easy to compute with. How good is it as a stand-in for
the curve, and how fast does it get worse as you move away?

## The linearization

The tangent to $f$ at $a$ is the line

$$
L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a).
$$

Used to estimate $f$ near $a$, it is called the linearization of $f$ at $a$. For <C c="blue">$f(x) = \sqrt{x}$</C> at $a = 4$, $f(4) = 2$ and $f'(4) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{4}} = \frac{1}{4}$, so <C c="teal">$L(x) = 2 + \frac{1}{4}(x - 4)$</C>. The <StepLink step="fit">zoom</StepLink> shows
why it works: near $A$ the two graphs are almost the same line, which is what <Term id="locally-linear">locally linear</Term> means.

## Differentials

Leibniz's $dx$ and $dy$ get a meaning of their own here. Take $dx$ to be any change in $x$, a
real number, positive or negative. Then define

$$
dy = f'(x)\,dx.
$$

$dy$ is the change in height along the tangent, while
$\Delta y = f(x + dx) - f(x)$ is the change along the curve (<StepLink step="differentials">drag
$dx$</StepLink>). When $dx \neq 0$, dividing gives $\frac{dy}{dx} = f'(x)$: the symbol
from <Term id="leibniz-notation">Leibniz notation</Term> is also a true quotient, of two
differentials. It still means the limit of $\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}$ too; the two readings
agree.

The differentials are taken in a variable. For the area of a disk, $A = \pi r^2$, they are taken
in $r$: $dA = 2\pi r\,dr$.

## An estimate by hand

To estimate $\sqrt{4.1}$, step $dx = 0.1$ from $a = 4$:

$$
\sqrt{4.1} \approx L(4.1) = 2 + \frac{1}{4} \cdot 0.1 = 2.025.
$$

The true value is $2.024846\ldots$, so the error is about $0.00016$. Physics uses the same idea
for a pendulum: the tangent to $\sin\theta$ at $0$ is $\theta$ itself, so $\sin\theta \approx
\theta$ for small angles, and the equation of motion becomes one that can be solved exactly.

## How fast the error shrinks

The error is $\Delta y - dy$. That it is small is not the point; that it is small compared with
$dx$ is. Write

$$
\Delta y = dy + \varepsilon\,dx, \qquad \varepsilon = \frac{\Delta y}{dx} - f'(x).
$$

As $dx \to 0$, $\frac{\Delta y}{dx} \to f'(x)$ by the definition of the derivative, so
$\varepsilon \to 0$. Being <Term id="differentiable">differentiable</Term> means exactly this: the
change $\Delta y$ is a linear part $f'(x)\,dx$ plus an error that vanishes faster than $dx$.

When $f''$ also exists, the error shrinks like $dx^2$:

$$
\frac{\Delta y - dy}{dx^2} \to \frac{f''(a)}{2}.
$$

In <StepLink step="error">the zoom that follows $dx$</StepLink>, this ratio settles at
$-\frac{1}{64} = -0.015625$, since $f''(4) = -\frac{1}{32}$ for $\sqrt{x}$. The sign says the
tangent lies above the curve. Halve $dx$ and
the error drops to a quarter. The proof of this limit uses the Taylor remainder, which comes in
Calculus 2; the numbers on the stage are evidence, not a proof.

## Why the tangent is the best line

Any line through $A$ with slope $m$ predicts $f(a) + m\,dx$. Its error divided by $dx$ is

$$
\frac{\Delta y - m\,dx}{dx} = \frac{\Delta y}{dx} - m \to f'(a) - m.
$$

That limit is $0$ only for $m = f'(a)$. <StepLink step="best">Every other slope</StepLink> leaves
an error of the same size as $dx$, so the tangent is the only line that fits better and better as
you zoom in. This is the sense in which it is the best linear approximation.

## Errors in measurements

A radius measured as $r = 10$ cm with an error up to $dr = 0.1$ cm gives an area error up to

$$
dA = 2\pi r\,dr = 2\pi \cdot 10 \cdot 0.1 \approx 6.28\ \text{cm}^2.
$$

The units follow the notation: centimeters times centimeters. <StepLink step="ring">The
ring</StepLink> shows the picture: the exact change $\Delta A$ is the ring, and $dA$ is its
circumference times its width, short of the true ring by $\pi\,dr^2$. Dividing by $A = \pi r^2$
gives the relative error, $\frac{dA}{A} = 2\frac{dr}{r}$: a 1% error in the radius is about a 2%
error in the area.

## Where this goes

Linear approximation runs through the rest of this unit and beyond: L'Hôpital's rule compares two
linearizations, Newton's method follows the tangent to a root, and in many variables the tangent
plane and the gradient do the same job. Gradient descent, the method that trains neural networks,
takes small steps because the linear prediction of the loss is only trustworthy for small steps.
