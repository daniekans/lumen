The item [Extrema](/mathematics/calculus-1/applications-of-derivatives/extrema) found the largest and smallest values of a function
that was given. In a real problem nobody gives the function: there is a quantity to make as large
or as small as possible, and some rule that ties its parts together. Most of the work is building
the function. The calculus at the end is the closed interval method from that item.

## The method

1. **Variables.** Draw the situation and name every quantity that changes. Write the
   <Term id="objective-function">objective</Term>, the quantity to optimize, in terms of them.
2. **Constraint.** Write the rule that ties them together (a fixed volume, a fixed sheet), and use
   it to remove all but one variable.
3. **Interval.** Find the values the remaining variable can take. The ends of this interval are
   part of the answer.
4. **Optimize.** Find the <Term id="critical-point">critical points</Term> inside the interval and
   compare the objective there and at the ends (or, on an open interval, at the edges in the limit).
5. **Check.** Units, a second look at the ends, and whether the answer makes sense.

## An open box

Cut a square of side $x$ from each corner of a $12$ by $12$ sheet of cardboard and fold up the
sides, as in the step <StepLink step="box-model">model: an open box</StepLink>. The box has a
square base of side $12 - 2x$ and height $x$, so

$$
V(x) = x(12 - 2x)^2, \qquad 0 \le x \le 6.
$$

The constraint (the sheet is $12$ wide) is already used: it is why the base is $12 - 2x$. The
interval comes from the picture: a cut cannot be negative, and the base cannot be. Both ends give
$V = 0$, a box with no sides or no base, so the maximum is inside.

By the product and chain rules,

$$
V'(x) = (12 - 2x)^2 - 4x(12 - 2x) = (12 - 2x)(12 - 6x),
$$

which is $0$ at $x = 2$ and at $x = 6$. The candidates are $V(0) = 0$, $V(2) = 128$ and
$V(6) = 0$, so the largest box, $128$ cubic centimeters, has $x = 2$
(step <StepLink step="box-optimize">optimize</StepLink>). The units check: $x$ in centimeters, $V$ in
cubic centimeters, $V'$ in square centimeters (volume per centimeter of cut).

## A can with the least metal

A can must hold $330$ cubic centimeters, and its metal (two disks and the side) should be as
little as possible. Two variables, $r$ and $h$:

$$
A = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r h, \qquad \pi r^2 h = 330.
$$

The constraint can remove either one. Solving for $h$ is easier, $h = \frac{330}{\pi r^2}$, so
keep $r$ and differentiate in $r$:

$$
A(r) = 2\pi r^2 + \frac{660}{r}, \qquad A'(r) = 4\pi r - \frac{660}{r^2}.
$$

The choice of variable changes the formulas, not the answer: keeping $h$ gives the same can. What
matters is that the objective depends on one variable when you differentiate. Differentiating
$A = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r h$ in $r$ while treating $h$ as a constant would be wrong, because the
constraint makes $h$ change whenever $r$ does.

$A'(r) = 0$ gives $r^3 = \frac{165}{\pi}$, so $r \approx 3.74$ cm and $h \approx 7.49$ cm. Here
$r$ can be any positive number: the interval $(0, \infty)$ is open and has no ends to compare.
Instead, $A(r) \to \infty$ at both edges (a thin can has a huge side, a flat one huge disks), and
$A' < 0$ before the critical point and $A' > 0$ after it. By
the <Term id="first-derivative-test">first derivative test</Term>, the one critical point is the
absolute minimum.

At that point $h = 2r$: the best can is as tall as it is wide
(step <StepLink step="can">a can with the least metal</StepLink>). Real cans are taller, about $3.3$ cm in
radius. The model leaves things out: the lids are thicker than the side, a narrow can fits a hand,
and a tall can shows more label. The graph also shows why the cost is small: near a minimum $A'(r)
\approx 0$, so moving away from it changes $A$ slowly. The real can uses about $268$ square
centimeters instead of $264$, $1.5$ percent more.

## The lifeguard's path

A lifeguard at $G$, $30$ m from the shore, sees a swimmer at $S$, $20$ m out and $60$ m along the
shore. Running on sand is $6$ m/s, swimming is $2$ m/s. Entering the water $x$ meters along
the shore takes

$$
T(x) = \frac{\sqrt{x^2 + 30^2}}{6} + \frac{\sqrt{(60 - x)^2 + 20^2}}{2}, \qquad 0 \le x \le 60.
$$

The straight line $GS$ is the shortest path, but it is not the fastest: it swims too much. In
the step <StepLink step="lifeguard">the lifeguard's path</StepLink> the best entry point is
$x \approx 53.9$ m, with $T \approx 20.7$ seconds against $23.4$ for the straight line.

$T'(x) = 0$ has no tidy solution, so the number comes from a computer. The equation itself says
something:

$$
T'(x) = \frac{x}{6\sqrt{x^2 + 30^2}} - \frac{60 - x}{2\sqrt{(60 - x)^2 + 20^2}} = 0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{\sin\theta_1}{6} = \frac{\sin\theta_2}{2},
$$

where $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ are the angles of the two legs with the line perpendicular to the
shore. This is Snell's law for light passing from air into water: light takes the fastest path, so
it bends at the surface for the same reason.

## The mean as least squares

Given data $y_1, \dots, y_n$, which single number $c$ represents them best? One answer: the $c$
that makes the total squared misfit smallest,

$$
S(c) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - c)^2.
$$

In the step <StepLink step="mean">the mean as least squares</StepLink> each misfit is
a <C c="red">square</C>, and $S(c)$ is their total area. Differentiate in $c$ (the data are fixed):

$$
S'(c) = -2\sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - c) = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad c = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} y_i = \bar{y}.
$$

$S''(c) = 2n > 0$, so this is a minimum: the mean is
the <Term id="least-squares">least squares</Term> fit by a constant. For $2, 3, 7, 8, 10$ it is $6$,
with $S = 46$.

Squares punish large misfits heavily. Replacing $10$ by $20$ moves the mean from $6$ to $8$; the
median, which minimizes $\sum \lvert y_i - c \rvert$ instead, stays at $7$.

$S$ is the first loss function of this course. Machine learning fits a model by choosing its
parameters to minimize a loss like $S$, a sum of squared misfits over the data. With many
parameters the condition $S' = 0$ becomes "every partial derivative is $0$", and when it cannot be
solved by hand the minimum is found step by step (gradient descent, in Multivariable Calculus and
the item [Backpropagation](/machine-learning/neural-networks/training/backpropagation)).

## Checks

- **Units.** The answer's units match the question: centimeters for a cut, seconds for a time.
- **Ends.** On a closed interval, compare the ends. On an open interval, see what the objective
  does at its edges; a single critical point with the right sign change is then the answer.
- **Second look.** A critical point can be a maximum when a minimum was asked for. The first or
  second derivative test settles it.
- **Sense.** $h = 2r$, a box cut of a sixth of the sheet, a path that runs more where running is
  fast. A strange answer usually means a wrong model, not a wrong derivative.
