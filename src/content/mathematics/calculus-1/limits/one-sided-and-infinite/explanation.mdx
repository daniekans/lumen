A <Term id="limit">limit</Term> asks for one value that $f(x)$ approaches from both sides. Three
things can get in the way: the two sides approach different values, the values grow without bound,
or the question is about $x$ growing without bound instead of approaching a number.

## One-sided limits

Restrict $x$ to one side of $a$ and the <Term id="one-sided-limit">one-sided limits</Term> are

$$
\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) \quad \text{(from the left, } x < a\text{)}, \qquad \lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) \quad \text{(from the right, } x > a\text{)}.
$$

The two-sided limit exists exactly when both one-sided limits exist and are equal:

$$
\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L \iff \lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = L = \lim_{x \to a^+} f(x).
$$

In <StepLink step="one-sided">the first step</StepLink> the function is $x + 1$ left of $1$ and
$(2 + k) - (x - 1)$ from $1$ on. The left limit is $2$ and the right limit is $2 + k$, so for
$k \neq 0$ the graph jumps and $\lim_{x \to 1} f(x)$ does not exist, even though $f(1) = 2 + k$ is
defined. Piecewise functions, step functions and $\frac{|x|}{x}$ at $0$ all behave this way.

## Infinite limits

Near $x = 2$, $\frac{1}{x - 2}$ divides $1$ by a tiny number. Left of $2$ the denominator is
negative, right of $2$ positive:

$$
\lim_{x \to 2^-} \frac{1}{x - 2} = -\infty, \qquad \lim_{x \to 2^+} \frac{1}{x - 2} = \infty.
$$

These are <Term id="infinite-limit">infinite limits</Term>. Writing $\infty$ does not make the limit
a number; it records that the values grow without bound, with a sign. The line $x = 2$ is a
vertical <Term id="asymptote">asymptote</Term>. With a square, $\frac{1}{(x - 2)^2}$, both sides are positive
and $\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{1}{(x - 2)^2} = \infty$. The sign test is the whole technique: a nonzero
number over something tending to $0$ gives $\pm\infty$, and the signs on each side decide which.

Some infinite limits come from one side only, because the other side has no meaning. A plant that
removes $p$ percent of a pollutant from its waste water might pay, in thousands of dollars,

$$
C(p) = \frac{50p}{100 - p}, \qquad 0 \le p < 100.
$$

Removing $90\%$ costs $450$, $99\%$ costs $4950$, and $99.9\%$ costs $49\,950$. Each extra nine
multiplies the cost by about ten, and

$$
\lim_{p \to 100^-} \frac{50p}{100 - p} = \infty:
$$

the last bit of pollutant is the most expensive, and removing all of it is out of reach at any
price. The limit from the right is not asked, since $p$ cannot exceed $100$.

## Limits at infinity

A <Term id="limit-at-infinity">limit at infinity</Term> lets $x$ grow without bound:

$$
\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{2x^2 - 3x}{x^2 + 1} = 2.
$$

The values settle toward the <Term id="horizontal-asymptote">horizontal asymptote</Term> $y = 2$
($f(1000) \approx 1.997$), and so they do as $x \to -\infty$. A graph may cross a horizontal
asymptote; this one does at $x = -\frac{2}{3}$. The asymptote is about
the <Term id="end-behavior">end behavior</Term> only.

## Dominant terms

To find a limit at infinity of a rational function, divide the top and the bottom by the highest
power of $x$ in the denominator:

$$
\frac{2x^2 - 3x}{x^2 + 1} = \frac{2 - \frac{3}{x}}{1 + \frac{1}{x^2}} \to \frac{2 - 0}{1 + 0} = 2.
$$

Every term with $x$ in a denominator fades, so only the leading terms count:

| Degrees    | Example                     | Limit at $\pm\infty$ |
| ---------- | --------------------------- | -------------------- |
| top lower  | $\frac{3x + 1}{x^2 + 1}$    | $0$                  |
| equal      | $\frac{2x^2 - 3x}{x^2 + 1}$ | $2$, the ratio       |
| top higher | $\frac{x^3 + x}{x^2 + 4}$   | $\pm\infty$          |

The same idea works with roots: $\sqrt{4x^2 + 1}$ behaves like $\sqrt{4x^2} = 2|x|$, which is
$2x$ for $x > 0$ and $-2x$ for $x < 0$, so the two infinities can give different limits. The
Growth rates lesson extends dominant terms to logarithms, powers and exponentials.
