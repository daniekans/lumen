The <Term id="limit-laws">limit laws</Term> and algebra handle polynomials, roots and rational
functions. Some limits need a different idea: $\frac{\sin x}{x}$ at $0$ gives
$\frac{0}{0}$, and no factoring removes it. The squeeze theorem settles such limits by trapping
the function between two simpler ones. Three limits found this way come up so often that they
are worth knowing by heart.

## The squeeze theorem

If $g(x) \le f(x) \le h(x)$ for every $x$ near $a$ (except possibly at $a$) and

$$
\lim_{x \to a} g(x) = L = \lim_{x \to a} h(x),
$$

then $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$. The function has nowhere else to go.

In <StepLink step="squeeze">the first step</StepLink>, $\sin\frac{1}{x}$ swings between $-1$ and
$1$ ever faster as $x \to 0$, so it has no limit there. Multiplying by $x$ tames it:
$|x \sin\frac{1}{x}| \le |x|$, so <C c="blue">$x \sin\frac{1}{x}$</C> lies between <C c="teal">$-|x|$ and $|x|$</C>, and

$$
\lim_{x \to 0} x \sin\frac{1}{x} = 0.
$$

The same pattern works whenever a bounded factor multiplies something that tends to $0$.

## sin x over x

Take an angle $x$ in radians between $0$ and $\frac{\pi}{2}$ on the unit circle, as in <StepLink step="areas">the second step</StepLink>. Three nested shapes share the corner $O$ and the
point $A = (1, 0)$:

$$
\underbrace{\frac{\sin x}{2}}_{\text{triangle } OAP} \le \underbrace{\frac{x}{2}}_{\text{sector}} \le \underbrace{\frac{\tan x}{2}}_{\text{triangle } OAT}.
$$

Multiply by $\frac{2}{\sin x}$, which is positive, and take reciprocals, which flips the
inequalities:

$$
\cos x \le \frac{\sin x}{x} \le 1.
$$

Both bounds tend to $1$, so by the squeeze theorem

$$
\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} = 1.
$$

For negative $x$ the quotient is the same as at $-x$, so the limit is two-sided. The sector's
area is $\frac{x}{2}$ only when $x$ is in radians; in degrees the limit would be
$\frac{\pi}{180}$. This limit is why calculus measures angles in radians, and it gives
$\sin' = \cos$ in the Derivatives unit. Related limits follow from it, for example
$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin 3x}{x} = 3 \lim_{u \to 0} \frac{\sin u}{u} = 3$ with $u = 3x$.

## The slope of e^x at 0

The quotient $\frac{b^h - 1}{h}$ is the slope of the secant of $b^x$ from $x = 0$ to $x = h$. In <StepLink step="exponential">the exponential step</StepLink> it tends to about $0.693$ for $b = 2$
and $1.099$ for $b = 3$. Somewhere between them is the base whose slope at $0$ is exactly $1$, and
that base is the <Term id="euler-number">number $e$</Term>:

$$
\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{e^h - 1}{h} = 1.
$$

In general the limit is $\ln b$. That $e^x$ is its own slope everywhere follows from this one
limit, in the Derivatives unit.

## (1 + 1/n)^n

The compound interest formula gives the third limit:

$$
\lim_{n \to \infty} \left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^n = e \approx 2.71828.
$$

The base tends to $1$ and the exponent to $\infty$, and the two effects balance: $1^\infty$ is an <Term id="indeterminate-form">indeterminate form</Term>, like $\frac{0}{0}$. With $t = \frac{1}{n}$
the same limit reads $\lim_{t \to 0} (1 + t)^{1/t} = e$, which is
how <StepLink step="compound">the last step</StepLink> draws it. From either side the values approach
$e$; at $n = 50$ the gap is still about $0.027$. The more general form is
$\lim_{n \to \infty} \left(1 + \frac{a}{n}\right)^n = e^a$.
