Two skills: continuity with the intermediate value theorem, and the squeeze theorem with the
standard limits.

## Continuity

$f$ is <Term id="continuity">continuous</Term> at $a$ when $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)$. For a
piecewise function, find the limit from each side and the value at the point:

- the one-sided limits differ: a jump;
- they agree but the value differs or is missing: removable;
- the values grow without bound: infinite (for a rational function, a factor of the bottom that
  does not cancel);
- the values swing forever without settling, as $\sin\frac{1}{x - a}$ does: oscillating. A factor
  that tends to $0$, as in $(x - a)\sin\frac{1}{x - a}$, damps the swings, and the squeeze
  theorem gives the limit $0$.

To make a piecewise function continuous, set the limit of one piece equal to the value of the
other at the meeting point and solve for the constant.

## Roots

By the <Term id="intermediate-value-theorem">intermediate value theorem</Term>, a continuous
function that changes sign on $[a, b]$ has a root there. Evaluate at whole numbers until the sign
changes. To solve an equation such as $\cos x = x$, move everything to one side,
$g(x) = \cos x - x$, and look for a sign change of $g$.

Bisection halves the interval at each step, so after $k$ steps an interval of width $w$ has width
$\frac{w}{2^k}$. For an accuracy, find the smallest $k$ with $\frac{w}{2^k}$ below it.

## Standard limits

The <Term id="squeeze-theorem">squeeze theorem</Term>: bounds with a common limit force it on
the function between them. When a bounded factor such as $\sin\frac{1}{x}$ or $\sin x$ appears,
build the bounds yourself from $-1 \le \sin \le 1$. The three standard limits, each with
$u \to 0$ or $n \to \infty$:

$$
\frac{\sin u}{u} \to 1, \qquad \frac{e^u - 1}{u} \to 1, \qquad \left(1 + \frac{a}{n}\right)^n \to e^a.
$$

Rewrite the expression so that one of them appears with the same $u$ on top and bottom, and pull
the constants out: $\frac{\sin 4x}{3x} = \frac{4}{3} \cdot \frac{\sin 4x}{4x} \to \frac{4}{3}$.
The third gives continuous compounding: at a yearly rate $r$ for $t$ years, $P\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt} \to Pe^{rt}$.

## Answering

Answers are numbers; exact forms such as $\frac{4}{3}$ or $e^{2}$ are accepted.
