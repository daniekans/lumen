Many limits at infinity compare two functions that both grow without bound, such as
$\frac{x^{10}}{2^x}$ or $\frac{\ln x}{\sqrt{x}}$. Their limit depends on which one grows faster
in the long run. The families of functions line up in a fixed order, and knowing it settles such
limits at a glance:

$$
\ln x \ll x^p \ll b^x \ll x! \qquad (p > 0,\ b > 1),
$$

where $f \ll g$ means $\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = 0$.

## A logarithmic scale

On a <Term id="logarithmic-scale">logarithmic scale</Term> the height is $\log_{10} y$, so each
step up multiplies by $10$. An exponential becomes a straight line, since
$\log_{10} b^x = x \log_{10} b$, as in <StepLink step="log-scale">the first step</StepLink>.
The scale fits $2^{40} \approx 1.1 \cdot 10^{12}$ and $10^{40}$ on one picture, which an ordinary
graph cannot.

## Powers against exponentials

A power gives $\log_{10} x^p = p \log_{10} x$, which bends over: multiplying $x$ by $10$ adds
only $p$ to the height, while the line of $2^x$ keeps the same slope. However large $p$ is, the
line passes the curve for good, in <StepLink step="powers">the second step</StepLink>:

| $p$ | $2^x > x^p$ for every $x$ past |
| --- | ------------------------------ |
| 2   | $4$                            |
| 3   | about $9.94$                   |
| 5   | about $22.4$                   |
| 10  | about $58.8$                   |

So $\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{x^p}{b^x} = 0$ for every power $p$ and base $b > 1$. A bigger $p$
or a base close to $1$ only delays the crossing: $1.1^x$ passes $x^{10}$ near $x \approx 685$.

## Logarithms against powers

The logarithm is the inverse of the exponential, and it loses to every power in the same way:

$$
\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{\ln x}{x^p} = 0 \quad \text{for every } p > 0.
$$

With $x = e^t$ this is $\frac{t}{e^{pt}} \to 0$, the previous rule in disguise. Small powers win
late: $x^{0.1}$ stays above $\ln x$ only from about $x \approx 3.4 \cdot 10^{15}$
(<StepLink step="logarithm">the logarithm step</StepLink>).

## The factorial

$n! = 1 \cdot 2 \cdots n$ multiplies by a growing factor at each step, $n + 1$, while $b^n$
always multiplies by $b$. Once $n + 1 > b$, the factorial pulls ahead by a growing factor each
step, so $\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{b^n}{n!} = 0$. Against $10^n$ it takes until $n = 25$
(<StepLink step="factorial">the factorial step</StepLink>). The curve between whole numbers is the
gamma function, $\Gamma(x + 1)$, which extends the factorial.

## Dominant terms

In a sum, the fastest-growing term decides the behavior at infinity. Divide the top and the bottom
by the fastest term in the denominator, as with rational functions:

$$
\frac{3 \cdot 2^x + x^4}{2^x + x^2} = \frac{3 + \frac{x^4}{2^x}}{1 + \frac{x^2}{2^x}} \to \frac{3 + 0}{1 + 0} = 3.
$$

"In the long run" can take a while: in <StepLink step="dominant-terms">the dominant terms
step</StepLink> the ratio climbs to about $16$ near $x = 7$ before the exponentials take over.

## Big-O

Often only the order of growth matters, not the constant. <Term id="big-o">Big-O</Term> notation
writes $f(x) = O(g(x))$ when there are constants $C$ and $x_0$ with

$$
|f(x)| \le C\, |g(x)| \quad \text{for all } x \ge x_0.
$$

When $\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}$ has a finite limit, $f = O(g)$. For $f(x) = 3x^2 + 5x + 2$ the ratio
$\frac{f(x)}{x^2}$ tends to $3$, so $f = O(x^2)$ with any $C > 3$; with $C = 4$, the bound holds
from $x_0 \approx 5.37$ (<StepLink step="big-o">the last step</StepLink>). Big-O drops constant
factors and lower terms: $2^{x + 1} = O(2^x)$, but $2^{2x} = 4^x$ is not $O(2^x)$. It is the
language of algorithm costs, where sorting $n$ rows is $O(n \log n)$ and a nested loop join
$O(nm)$.
