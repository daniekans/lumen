A <Term id="limit">limit</Term> ignores the value at the point. Continuity asks for the case where
the two agree: the graph can be drawn through $a$ without lifting the pen. It is the property that
lets <Term id="direct-substitution">direct substitution</Term> work, and on an interval it
guarantees that equations have solutions.

## The definition

$f$ is continuous at $a$ when

$$
\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a).
$$

That one equation holds three conditions, checked in <StepLink step="definition">the first step</StepLink>:

1. $f(a)$ is defined.
2. $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ exists.
3. The two are equal.

A function is continuous on an interval when it is continuous at every point of it (at an
endpoint, with the one-sided limit from inside). Polynomials are continuous everywhere; rational,
root, exponential, logarithmic and trigonometric functions are continuous wherever they are
defined. By the <Term id="limit-laws">limit laws</Term>, sums, products and quotients (where the
denominator is not $0$) of continuous functions are continuous. So are compositions: if $g$ is
continuous at $a$ and $f$ is continuous at $g(a)$, then

$$
\lim_{x \to a} f(g(x)) = f\left(\lim_{x \to a} g(x)\right) = f(g(a)),
$$

the composition rule of the Limit laws lesson with its condition named. More generally the
limit passes inside $f$ whenever $\lim g = b$ exists and $f$ is continuous at $b$.

## Kinds of discontinuity

Each failed condition gives a different picture, as in <StepLink step="discontinuities">the second step</StepLink>:

| Kind        | Example at $a = 1$                     | What fails                                                      |
| ----------- | -------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| removable   | a hole, or a point moved off the curve | the limit exists but differs from $f(1)$ or $f(1)$ is undefined |
| jump        | a piecewise function that steps        | the one-sided limits differ                                     |
| infinite    | $2 + \frac{1}{x - 1}$                  | the values grow without bound                                   |
| oscillating | $2 + \sin\frac{1}{x - 1}$              | the values never settle                                         |

The infinite example is continuous wherever it is defined, since $1$ is not in its domain. Calling
$1$ an infinite discontinuity names the break in the graph there: the first condition fails, and
the values blow up on both sides. The two statements describe the same function, one on its
domain and one at a point outside it.

A removable discontinuity is the mild one: redefine $f(a)$ as the limit and the function becomes
continuous. That is how $\frac{\sin x}{x}$ with the value $1$ at $0$ becomes continuous
everywhere, and how a <Term id="hole">hole</Term> in a rational function gets filled.

## Matching pieces

A piecewise function built from continuous pieces can only break where the pieces meet. To make

$$
f(x) = \begin{cases} x^2 + c & x < 2 \\ 3x - 1 & x \ge 2 \end{cases}
$$

continuous, the left limit $4 + c$ must equal $f(2) = 5$, so $c = 1$
(<StepLink step="parameter">the parameter step</StepLink>).

## The intermediate value theorem

If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and $N$ is any number between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, then there is
at least one $c$ in $(a, b)$ with

$$
f(c) = N.
$$

A continuous graph cannot get from one height to another without passing every height in between.
In <StepLink step="ivt">the theorem's step</StepLink>, $f(x) = x^3 - x - 1$ has $f(1) = -1$ and
$f(2) = 5$, so it takes every value between $-1$ and $5$ on $(1, 2)$. Continuity is essential: a
jump can skip values. The theorem says a $c$ exists, not where it is or how many there are.

Its most common use is $N = 0$: when $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ have opposite signs, $f$ has a root in
$(a, b)$.

## Bisection

The theorem turns into an algorithm. Start with an interval where $f$ changes sign, check the sign
at the midpoint $m$, and keep the half whose ends still differ in sign:

$$
[a, b] \to [a, m] \text{ or } [m, b].
$$

The root stays trapped, and the interval halves each time, so after $k$ steps its width is
$\frac{b - a}{2^k}$. In <StepLink step="bisection">the last step</StepLink> the root of
$x^3 - x - 1$ is about $1.3247$; ten steps narrow $[1, 2]$ to a width under $0.001$. Bisection is
slow next to Newton's method (in Applications of derivatives) but it never fails on a continuous
function with a sign change. The same halving idea is binary search over a sorted array.
