Implicit differentiation treated $y$ as a function of $x$. Here every quantity is a function of
time $t$, and the equation that links them holds at every moment. Differentiating it in $t$ links
their rates: knowing some of them at an instant gives the others.

## A sliding ladder

A ladder $5$ meters long leans on a wall, as in the step <StepLink step="ladder">a sliding
ladder</StepLink>. Its foot is $x$ meters from the wall and its top $y$ meters up, both functions
of $t$, and at every moment

$$
x^2 + y^2 = 25.
$$

Both sides are the same function of $t$, so they have the same derivative.
By the <Term id="chain-rule">chain rule</Term>, $\frac{d}{dt}\left(x^2\right) = 2x\,\frac{dx}{dt}$, so

$$
2x\,\frac{dx}{dt} + 2y\,\frac{dy}{dt} = 0, \qquad \frac{dy}{dt} = -\frac{x}{y}\,\frac{dx}{dt}.
$$

With the foot sliding out at $\frac{dx}{dt} = 0.5$ meters per second, the top moves at
$-\frac{3}{4} \cdot 0.5 = -0.375$ meters per second when $x = 3$ (and $y = 4$). The sign says it
falls. As $y$ gets small the factor $\frac{x}{y}$ grows, so the top falls faster and faster, while
the foot keeps the same speed.

## The method

1. Draw the situation and name the quantities that change, as functions of $t$. Write down the
   rates you know and the rate you want.
2. Find an equation that links the quantities at every moment (Pythagoras, a volume formula,
   similar triangles, a trigonometric ratio). Use any fixed relation to remove extra quantities.
3. Differentiate both sides in $t$, with the chain rule on every changing quantity.
4. Only now substitute the values at the instant asked about, and solve for the unknown rate.

Substituting before step 3 is the usual mistake: $x = 3$ holds at one instant, so it has no rate of
change to differentiate.

## Filling a cone

Water flows into a cone with its tip down, top radius $3$ and height $6$, at $2\pi$ cubic meters per
second. In the step <StepLink step="tank">filling a cone</StepLink> the water is a smaller cone of
depth $h$ and surface radius $r$. Similar triangles give $\frac{r}{h} = \frac{3}{6}$, so
$r = \frac{h}{2}$ at every moment, and the volume is

$$
V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h = \frac{\pi h^3}{12}.
$$

Differentiate in $t$:

$$
\frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{\pi h^2}{4}\,\frac{dh}{dt}, \qquad \frac{dh}{dt} = \frac{4}{\pi h^2} \cdot 2\pi = \frac{8}{h^2}.
$$

The factor $\frac{\pi h^2}{4} = \pi r^2$ is the area of the surface: a thin layer of depth
$\Delta h$ on top has volume about $\pi r^2\,\Delta h$. The same inflow spread over a wider surface
raises the level more slowly. At $h = 4$ the level rises at $\frac{8}{16} = 0.5$ meters per second.

## An angle of elevation

A balloon rises straight up at $1$ meter per second, $4$ meters from an observer on the ground, as
in the step <StepLink step="balloon">an angle of elevation</StepLink>. The angle of elevation
$\theta$ and the height $h$ satisfy

$$
\tan\theta = \frac{h}{4}.
$$

Differentiate in $t$, using $\frac{d}{d\theta}\tan\theta = \frac{1}{\cos^2\theta}$ and the chain
rule:

$$
\frac{1}{\cos^2\theta}\,\frac{d\theta}{dt} = \frac{1}{4}\,\frac{dh}{dt}, \qquad \frac{d\theta}{dt} = \frac{\cos^2\theta}{4}.
$$

At $h = 4$ the angle is $\frac{\pi}{4}$, $\cos^2\theta = \frac{1}{2}$, and $\theta$ grows at
$\frac{1}{8}$ radian per second. The balloon keeps its speed, but the angle turns more and more
slowly as it rises: with the angle in radians, the rate falls from $\frac{1}{4}$ toward $0$.
