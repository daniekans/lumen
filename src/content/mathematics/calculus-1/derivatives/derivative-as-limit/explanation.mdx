The slope of a straight line is rise over run. A curve has no single slope, but we can still ask how
steep it is at one point. The derivative answers that question with a <Term id="limit">limit</Term>.

## Average rate of change

Pick a point $P = (x_0, f(x_0))$ and a second point $Q$ a horizontal distance $h$ away. The step
$h$ is the <Term id="increment">increment</Term> $\Delta x$ written with one letter, and the
change in height is $\Delta y = f(x_0 + h) - f(x_0)$. The line through both points is the
yellow <C c="yellow">secant</C>, and its slope is

$$
\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{f(x_0 + h) - f(x_0)}{h}.
$$

This is the average rate of change of $f$ between $x_0$ and $x_0 + h$.

## Instantaneous rate of change

Setting $h = 0$ gives $\frac{0}{0}$, which has no value. As in the Limits unit, we ask what the
quotient approaches instead:

$$
f'(x_0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x_0 + h) - f(x_0)}{h}.
$$

When this limit exists, $f$ is differentiable at $x_0$, and the line through $P$ with slope
$f'(x_0)$ is the <C c="teal">tangent</C>:

$$
y = f(x_0) + f'(x_0)(x - x_0).
$$

In the visualization the readouts converge: at $h = 0.001$ the secant slope and $f'(x_0)$ agree to
about three decimal places.

## Locally straight

Try <StepLink step="zoom">zooming in on $P$</StepLink>: it shows what the limit means geometrically. The gap
between the curve and its tangent shrinks faster than the distance from $P$, so under enough
magnification the two look like one line. A function with a derivative at $x_0$ is locally straight
there, and the tangent is the straight line it resembles. This idea returns as linear approximation
in the next unit, and later as the tangent plane and the gradient.

## The derivative is a function

Nothing in the definition depends on a particular $x_0$. Computing the limit at every point defines
a new function $f'$. For the curve shown here,

$$
f(x) = 0.1x^3 - 0.8x^2 + 1.6x + 1, \qquad f'(x) = 0.3x^2 - 1.6x + 1.6.
$$

Where $f$ climbs, $f'$ is positive. Where $f$ turns around, at $x = \frac{4}{3}$ and $x = 4$, the
tangent is flat and $f'$ crosses zero. Try <StepLink step="notation">dragging $P$ over those
points</StepLink>.

## Leibniz notation

Writing $y = f(x)$, Leibniz wrote the derivative as

$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}.
$$

The notation keeps the memory of the ratio it came from: $\Delta$, a finite change, becomes $d$ in
the limit. At this stage $\frac{dy}{dx}$ is one symbol for that limit, the same number as $f'(x)$.
Its value at a point is written $\left.\frac{dy}{dx}\right|_{x = x_0}$. Later items give $dy$ and
$dx$ their own meaning, as the rise and run along the tangent line, and the fraction then holds
literally.
