The slope of a straight line is rise over run. A curve has no single slope, but we can still ask how
steep it is at one point. The derivative answers that question by turning it into a limit.

## Average rate of change

Pick a point $P = (x_0, f(x_0))$ and a second point $Q$ a horizontal distance $h$ away. The line
through both is the <C c="yellow">secant</C>, and its slope is

$$
m_{\text{sec}} = \frac{f(x_0 + h) - f(x_0)}{h}.
$$

This is the average rate of change of $f$ between $x_0$ and $x_0 + h$.

## Instantaneous rate of change

Setting $h = 0$ directly gives $0/0$, which means nothing. Instead we look at what the secant slope
approaches as $h$ gets small:

$$
f'(x_0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x_0 + h) - f(x_0)}{h}.
$$

When this limit exists, the line through $P$ with slope $f'(x_0)$ is the <C c="teal">tangent</C>. In
the visualization the readouts converge: at $h = 0.001$ the secant slope and $f'(x_0)$ agree to
about three decimal places.

## The derivative is a function

Nothing in the definition depends on a particular $x_0$. Computing the limit at every point defines
a new function $f'$. For the curve shown here,

$$
f(x) = 0.1x^3 - 0.8x^2 + 1.6x + 1, \qquad f'(x) = 0.3x^2 - 1.6x + 1.6.
$$

Where $f$ climbs, $f'$ is positive. Where $f$ turns around, at $x = 4/3$ and $x = 4$,
the tangent is flat and $f'$ crosses zero. Try <StepLink step="function">dragging $P$ over those
points</StepLink>.
