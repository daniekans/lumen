The derivative was born as the mathematics of motion. A moving object has a position at every
moment, and its velocity and acceleration are derivatives of that position. This item follows a
ball thrown straight up, and reads Leibniz notation through its units.

## Position as a function of time

A ball leaves the ground at $19.6$ meters per second, straight up. Gravity slows it, and its height
in meters after $t$ seconds is

$$
s(t) = 19.6t - 4.9t^2.
$$

The step <StepLink step="position">position over time</StepLink> shows the ball on a vertical track
and, beside it, the graph of $s$. The graph is not the ball's path, which is a straight vertical
line: it records the height against time. The ball rises to $19.6$ meters at $t = 2$ and lands at
$t = 4$, when $s(4) = 78.4 - 78.4 = 0$.

## Average velocity

Over the interval from $t$ to $t + \Delta t$ the position changes by
$\Delta s = s(t + \Delta t) - s(t)$. The <Term id="difference-quotient">difference quotient</Term>

$$
\frac{\Delta s}{\Delta t} = \frac{s(t + \Delta t) - s(t)}{\Delta t}
$$

is the average velocity over the interval, the slope of the <C c="yellow">secant</C> in the
step <StepLink step="average">average velocity</StepLink>. Its units come from the quotient:
$\Delta s$ is in meters and $\Delta t$ in seconds, so $\frac{\Delta s}{\Delta t}$ is in meters per
second. Over the first second, $\frac{14.7 - 0}{1} = 14.7$ meters per second.

A car trip works the same way: $120$ kilometers in $1.5$ hours is an average of $80$ km/h, even
though the car stopped at lights and passed trucks on the way.

## Velocity is the derivative

The speedometer does not show an average over the trip. It shows the rate at one instant, which is
what the averages over shorter and shorter intervals approach. In the
step <StepLink step="instant">the speedometer reading</StepLink>, $\Delta t$ shrinks and the secant
turns into the <C c="teal">tangent</C>. The limit is the <Term id="velocity">velocity</Term>:

$$
v(t) = \frac{ds}{dt} = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta t} = 19.6 - 9.8t,
$$

by the power and sum rules. The <Term id="leibniz-notation">Leibniz notation</Term> keeps the
units visible: $\frac{ds}{dt}$ reads "change in $s$ over change in $t$", meters per second, because
each average in the limit has those units. At $t = 1$ the velocity is $9.8$ meters per second, less
than the average $14.7$ over the first second, since the ball was faster at the start of that
second than at its end.

## Velocity as a function

Computed at every instant, the velocity is a new function, drawn as the <C c="teal">teal
line</C> in the step <StepLink step="velocity">velocity as a function</StepLink>.
It is the <Term id="derivative-function">derivative function</Term> of $s$, and it carries the
direction of motion in its sign:

- $v > 0$: $s$ increases, the ball moves up.
- $v < 0$: $s$ decreases, the ball moves down.
- $v = 0$: the ball is at rest for an instant, here at the top, $t = 2$, where the tangent to $s$ is
  flat.

The speed is $\lvert v \rvert$, the reading without the direction. The ball leaves at $19.6$ meters
per second and lands with velocity $v(4) = -19.6$: the same speed, moving down.

## Acceleration, the second derivative

The velocity changes too. Its rate of change is the <Term id="acceleration">acceleration</Term>,
the derivative of the derivative:

$$
a(t) = \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{ds}{dt}\right) = \frac{d^2s}{dt^2}.
$$

For the ball, $a(t) = \frac{d}{dt}(19.6 - 9.8t) = -9.8$, the same at every moment: gravity, drawn
in <C c="orange">orange</C> in the step <StepLink step="acceleration">acceleration</StepLink>.

The units follow the notation again. $\frac{dv}{dt}$ is meters per second, per second: every second
the velocity drops by $9.8$ meters per second. That is written m/s², and the $dt^2$ in
$\frac{d^2s}{dt^2}$ is the same "seconds squared". The $2$s sit in different places because they
mean different things: in $d^2s$ the operator $\frac{d}{dt}$ is applied twice to $s$, and in $dt^2$
the time unit appears twice.

For any function whose derivative is itself differentiable, the derivative of $f'$ is
the <Term id="second-derivative">second derivative</Term> $f'' = (f')'$, written
$\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}$ for $y = f(x)$. It
measures how fast the slope changes. The unit on applications of derivatives reads it on a graph as
the way the curve bends.

## Speeding up or slowing down

A negative acceleration does not by itself mean slowing down. The speed $\lvert v \rvert$ grows when
the velocity moves away from $0$, which happens when $v$ and $a$ have the same sign:

| $v$ | $a$ | the ball                  |
| --- | --- | ------------------------- |
| $+$ | $-$ | moves up and slows down   |
| $-$ | $-$ | moves down and speeds up  |
| $+$ | $+$ | moves up and speeds up    |
| $-$ | $+$ | moves down and slows down |

The ball has $a = -9.8$ the whole time. Going up ($0 < t < 2$) it slows down; coming down
($2 < t < 4$) it speeds up, although the acceleration never changed.

## A general throw

With initial velocity $v_0$ and initial height $s_0$, the height is
$s(t) = s_0 + v_0t - 4.9t^2$ (in meters, with $t$ in seconds, near the Earth's surface and without
air resistance). Then $v(t) = v_0 - 9.8t$ and $a = -9.8$. Note what the derivatives recover:
$s(0) = s_0$, $v(0) = v_0$ and $a = -9.8$, the three facts the formula was built from. The ball
stops at the top when $v = 0$, at $t = \frac{v_0}{9.8}$. Going backward, from the acceleration to
the velocity and the position, is what integrals do in a later unit.
