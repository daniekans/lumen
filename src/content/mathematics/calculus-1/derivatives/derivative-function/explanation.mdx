The <Term id="derivative">derivative</Term> at one point is a number, the slope of the tangent
there. Computed at every point, those numbers form a function of their own.

## Plotting the slopes

For <C c="blue">$f(x) = 0.2x^3 - 1.2x^2 + 1.8x + 1$</C>, the slope at $x$ is

$$
f'(x) = 0.6x^2 - 2.4x + 1.8 = 0.6(x - 1)(x - 3).
$$

Now <StepLink step="trace">plot each slope as a height</StepLink>, and the points $(x, f'(x))$
draw the <C c="teal">graph of $f'$</C>. The two graphs share the $x$-axis, but their heights mean different
things: $f(x)$ is a value, $f'(x)$ a rate of change of that value.

## Reading one graph from the other

- Where $f$ rises, every tangent climbs, so $f'(x) > 0$: the <C c="green">rising intervals</C>
  $(0, 1)$ and $(3, 5.2)$.
- Where $f$ falls, $f'(x) < 0$: the <C c="red">falling interval</C> $(1, 3)$.
- At a turning point the tangent is flat, so $f'(x) = 0$. Here at $x = 1$ and $x = 3$.
- Where $f$ is steepest, $f'$ reaches an extreme value. At $x = 2$, $f$ falls fastest and $f'$ is
  at its lowest, $-0.6$.

The converse needs care: $f'(x) = 0$ means a flat tangent, not always a turning point. For $x^3$
the tangent at $0$ is flat, yet the function rises on both sides. The next unit sorts these cases.

## Where the derivative fails

$f'(a)$ exists when the difference quotient has a limit at $a$, the same number from both sides.
That limit can fail in three typical ways.

- **Corner.** For $|x - 2| + 1$ at $x = 2$, the quotient is $1$ for every $h > 0$ and $-1$ for
  every $h < 0$. The <Term id="one-sided-limit">one-sided limits</Term> exist but differ.
- **Cusp.** For $(x - 2)^{2/3} + 1$ the quotient is $h^{-1/3}$, which goes to $\infty$ from the
  right and to $-\infty$ from the left. The graph comes to a sharp point.
- **Vertical tangent.** For $\sqrt[3]{x - 2} + 2$ the quotient is $h^{-2/3}$, which goes to
  $\infty$ from both sides. The tangent exists as a line, but it is vertical, and a vertical line
  has no slope.

All three functions are continuous at $2$. Continuity does not guarantee a derivative. The
other direction does hold: if $f'(a)$ exists, then $f$ is continuous at $a$, because

$$
f(a + h) - f(a) = \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h} \cdot h \to f'(a) \cdot 0 = 0.
$$

## $\frac{d}{dx}$ as an operator

Differentiation is a machine that takes a function and gives back a function. Leibniz's
notation names the machine $\frac{d}{dx}$ and writes it in front of its input:

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\left(x^2\right) = 2x, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) = -\frac{1}{x^2},
\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\sqrt{x} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}.
$$

With $y = f(x)$, the forms $f'(x)$, $\frac{dy}{dx}$ and $\frac{d}{dx} f(x)$ all mean the same
function. The output can have a smaller domain than the input: $\sqrt{x}$ is defined at $0$, but
its derivative is not, since the tangent there is vertical. The next items find rules that give
the output without computing a limit each time.
