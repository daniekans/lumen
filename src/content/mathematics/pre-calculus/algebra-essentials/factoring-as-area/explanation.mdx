Factoring writes a sum as a product. Multiplying out $(x + 2)(x + 3)$ is routine; going back from
$x^2 + 5x + 6$ to the two factors takes a search. Area turns that search into a puzzle: the factors
are the sides of a rectangle made from the polynomial's tiles.

## Products as rectangles

The tiles are a <C c="blue">square of area $x^2$</C>, <C c="teal">strips of area $x$</C> (sides $x$
and $1$) and <C c="yellow">unit squares</C>. A rectangle with sides $x + p$ and $x + q$ holds one of
each kind of region:

$$
(x + p)(x + q) = x^2 + (p + q)x + pq.
$$

The middle coefficient is the number of strips, $p + q$; the constant is the block of units,
$p \times q$. Change $p$ and $q$ in <StepLink step="product">the first step</StepLink> and count.

## Factoring a trinomial

To factor $x^2 + bx + c$, look for two numbers with

$$
p + q = b, \qquad pq = c.
$$

List the factor pairs of $c$ and check their sums. For $x^2 + 7x + 12$ the pairs are
$1 \cdot 12$, $2 \cdot 6$ and $3 \cdot 4$, with sums $13$, $8$ and $7$, so
$x^2 + 7x + 12 = (x + 3)(x + 4)$. With a negative $b$ and a positive $c$ both numbers are negative:
$x^2 - 7x + 12 = (x - 3)(x - 4)$. With a negative $c$ the signs differ:
$x^2 + x - 12 = (x + 4)(x - 3)$. Tiles cannot show negative lengths, but the rule $p + q = b$,
$pq = c$ holds for any signs.

When the leading coefficient is not $1$, the same idea applies with more cases:
$2x^2 + 7x + 3 = (2x + 1)(x + 3)$. Multiply back to check.

## Common factors first

If every term shares a factor, take it out before anything else:

$$
3x^2 + 6x = 3x(x + 2), \qquad 2x^2 - 8 = 2(x^2 - 4) = 2(x + 2)(x - 2).
$$

$3(x^2 + 2x)$ is correct but not fully factored, because $x$ is still common to both terms.

## Difference of squares

$$
a^2 - b^2 = (a + b)(a - b).
$$

In <StepLink step="difference">the last step</StepLink>, cutting $q^2$ from the corner leaves a
piece of width $x - q$ and height $x$, plus a strip with sides $q$ and $x - q$. Turned and placed
on top, the strip adds height $q$, so the rectangle has sides $x - q$ and $x + q$. A sum of squares, $x^2 + q^2$, has no such
factorization with real numbers.
