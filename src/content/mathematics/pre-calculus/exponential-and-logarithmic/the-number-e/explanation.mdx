Any base $b > 0$ makes an <Term id="exponential-function">exponential function</Term>, but one base
appears whenever growth is continuous, and it makes the calculus of exponentials simplest. Two
questions lead to it: how far does compounding more often go, and which exponential has slope $1$
where it crosses the $y$-axis?

## Compounding

One unit at a yearly rate of $100\%$, compounded $n$ times a year, is multiplied by
$1 + \frac{1}{n}$ in each of the $n$ periods:

| $n$                              | $1$ | $4$     | $12$    | $365$   |
| -------------------------------- | --- | ------- | ------- | ------- |
| $\left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^n$ | $2$ | $2.441$ | $2.613$ | $2.715$ |

The values increase but stay bounded, and their limit is the <Term id="euler-number">number $e$</Term>:

$$
e = \lim_{n \to \infty} \left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^n \approx 2.71828.
$$

## The slope at zero

Every $b^x$ passes through $(0, 1)$. Its slope there is the limit of $\frac{b^h - 1}{h}$ as $h$
shrinks: about $0.693$ for $b = 2$ and $1.099$ for $b = 3$. The base whose slope is exactly $1$ is
$e$, as <StepLink step="slope-at-zero">the second step</StepLink> shows. The two definitions agree,
which the <Term id="derivative">derivative</Term> makes precise in Calculus 1.

## Slope equals height

Near any point $x$,

$$
e^{x + h} = e^x \cdot e^h,
$$

so the curve near $x$ is the curve near $0$ stretched vertically by $e^x$, and so is its slope:
$1 \cdot e^x$. The slope of $e^x$ equals its height everywhere. Geometrically, the tangent at
$(x, e^x)$ meets the $x$-axis at $x - 1$: its run is $1$ and its rise is the height. No other
base has this property, which is why $e^x$ is called the natural exponential.

## Continuous growth

Compounding a rate $r$ in $n$ periods a year gives $\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}$ after $t$
years, and as $n \to \infty$ this tends to $e^{rt}$. So <Term id="continuous-compounding">continuous growth</Term> at rate $r$ is

$$
A(t) = P e^{rt}.
$$

A negative $r$ is continuous decay. The model doubles when $e^{rd} = 2$, at $d = \frac{\ln 2}{r}$,
about $\frac{0.693}{r}$: at $r = 7\%$ a year, about $10$ years.
