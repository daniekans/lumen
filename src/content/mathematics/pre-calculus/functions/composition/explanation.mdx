Composition answers the question: what happens when the output of one function becomes the input of
another? The result is a new function, and most formulas in calculus are built this way.

## Definition

For functions $f$ and $g$, the composition $f \circ g$ (read "$f$ after $g$") is

$$
(f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x)).
$$

The function written on the right acts first. With $g(x) = x + 1$ and $f(u) = u^2$,

$$
(f \circ g)(x) = f(x + 1) = (x + 1)^2.
$$

The <C c="teal">first arrow</C> applies $g$, the <C c="blue">second</C> applies $f$ to the result.

## Order matters

Composing the other way gives a different function:

$$
(g \circ f)(x) = g(x^2) = x^2 + 1.
$$

At $x = 1$, $(f \circ g)(1) = 4$ but $(g \circ f)(1) = 2$. Switch the order in the <StepLink step="order">third step</StepLink> and watch the middle line rearrange.

## Computing a composition

Work from the inside out. To find $f(g(-3))$, first $g(-3) = -2$, then $f(-2) = 4$. To find the
formula, substitute the whole expression $g(x)$ for the variable of $f$, keeping it in parentheses.

## Domain

$x$ must be in the <Term id="domain">domain</Term> of $g$, and $g(x)$ must be in the domain of $f$. For example, with
$f(u) = \sqrt{u}$ and $g(x) = x - 2$, the composition $f(g(x)) = \sqrt{x - 2}$ needs $x \geq 2$,
even though $g$ accepts every real number.
